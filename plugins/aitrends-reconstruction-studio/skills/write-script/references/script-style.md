# The reconstruction voice — house style for narration

This guide is self-contained. It describes, with measurements and original examples, the voice of a calm, forensic
"what really happened" documentary: a single narrator walks the viewer through a real incident minute by minute,
with every claim sourced, every unknown admitted, and 3D reconstructions shown while the words land.
The examples below are invented to illustrate technique; never reuse them as facts.

## 1. The voice in one paragraph
Present tense for the event, past tense for what people said afterwards. Short declarative sentences, many fragments.
Concrete nouns, exact numbers, named sources. No adjectives of hype, no opinions, no moralising. Emotion is carried by
the facts and by people's own words, never by the narrator. The narrator is a careful guide who says out loud what is
not known. The tone is quiet and steady, closer to an investigator briefing a jury than a presenter selling a story.

## 2. Numbers that define the style (measured)
| Measure | Long-form 16:9 | Short 9:16 |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 1,800–2,700 words (12–18 min) | 160–260 words (60–90 s) |
| Delivery pace | ~145–155 wpm, unhurried | ~165–185 wpm |
| Sentence length | median 8–12 words; longest ≤ 30–35 | median 6–9 |
| Fragments (≤ 6 words) | 20–35% of sentences | 30–45% |
| Attributions ("says", "according to", "X reports", "told NBC") | ~2.5–3.5 per minute | 1–3 in total |
| Stated unknowns ("has not been made public", "Police haven't said…") | 4–8 per film | 1–2 |
| Questions | 3–7 per film, used as chapter pivots | 1 (the hook) |
| Pauses after sentences | ~0.7 s typical, up to ~1.1 s; longer only at chapter breaks | ~0.35 s |

## 3. Sentence craft
- **Lead with the concrete.** Start lines with a place, a time, a number, a person or an object.
  - *"Nine minutes past four. Platform two."*
  - *"Row 22. A nurse coming home from a night shift."*
- **Fragments for emphasis.** Times, numbers, lists of injuries, single objects.
  - *"Head, hands, neck."*
  - *"General emergency."*
  - *"Thirty-six riders."*
  - *"A safety tool."*
- **One idea per sentence.** Split anything with two clauses that both carry facts.
- **Quotes inline with the attribution in the middle.**
  - *"I pushed the door, she says, and it just gave."*
  - *"It felt like slow motion, he says."*
  - Keep quotes verbatim. Trim them only at natural boundaries, and never merge two quotes.
- **Attribution is woven, not bolted on.**
  - Lead with it: *"According to the county's own inspection report, …"*
  - Tuck it mid-sentence: *"…the bar, the state says, had been adjusted…"*
  - Tag it at the end: *"…before the train left, two riders told local news."*
- **Precision words for hedging, never vague ones.**
  - Say *"has not been published"*, *"no official has said"*, *"accounts differ"*, *"by his own account"*, *"the tracking data shows"*.
  - Avoid *"reportedly"* and *"allegedly"* unless that is literally the source's wording; name the source instead.
- **Contrast pairs and irony from facts, not commentary.**
  - *"A door built to keep danger out. And a morning when the danger was already inside."*
  - *"A man who repairs boilers for a living takes hold of the controls of an airliner."*
- **Deictic lines that drive the picture.** "This is…", "Here…", "Behind them, a door.", "On the other side of it…", "Here, the bar locks a few clicks high." Each one needs a matching reveal in the shot list.
- **Speakable numbers.**
  - Write them the way they are said: *"twenty-one minutes past eight"*, *"a quarter to ten"*, *"more than thirty thousand feet a minute"*.
  - Spell numbers out in the synthesis file whenever a voice might misread them.
- **Scale by comparison, briefly.** *"From the first wobble to a flat line: just over three minutes. It has lost more than half its height."*

## 4. Banned and discouraged
- **Hype:** shocking, terrifying, horrifying, chilling, insane, unbelievable, jaw-dropping, nightmare, tragic(ally), devastating, heart-breaking, gruesome, brutal, epic, "you won't believe".
- **AI tells:** delve, tapestry, testament to, a stark reminder, serves as a reminder, it is worth noting, it is important to note, in today's world, let's dive in, buckle up, game-changer, pivotal, underscores, intricate, nestled, unravel.
- **Narrator opinion:** "I think", "clearly", "obviously", "sadly", "luckily". The facts carry the weight.
- **Speculation presented as fact.** Possibilities are always labelled: *"Each is a possibility, not a finding."*
- **Rhetorical stacks of three questions in a row**, except once at most in a wrap-up.
- **Second person "you"**, except inside a quote or for one tactile explainer line: *"It clicks down over your legs and locks."*

## 5. Signature devices (use these deliberately)
1. **Altitude/place opener.** The first line puts the viewer at a precise point in space.
   - *"Forty metres under the river. Tunnel bore number three."*
   - *"Two hundred feet above the trees outside the city."*
2. **Witness in a seat.** Within 15 seconds, name one ordinary person at a precise spot, in a sentence that carries their words.
   - *"In car four, a teacher is riding with her brother. And then, she says, the lights go out."*
3. **The mystery line.** It closes the cold open.
   - *"Nobody in the cabin knows why. The answer is behind this door."*
   - *"Nobody on the platform knows what they have just seen."*
4. **The litany.** The film is previewed as a run of concrete fragments.
   - Start with "This is the story of…", then add "Of a…", "Of a…", "And…", 4–6 items.
   - Each item is a specific object, person or event that will pay off later, and the last is often an image.
   - End on the central question(s).
5. **The method line.** Say what the film is built on and what the 3D is.
   - *"This film is built on the official statements, the tracking data, and the words of the people who were there. Where they disagree, we'll say so."*
   - *"Nobody outside that room saw what happened. So what follows is a reconstruction."*
   - *"So we rebuilt the seat in 3D, from photos and the maker's patent, to show what each would look like."*
6. **The plant.** Introduce an object early, then tell the viewer to keep it in mind. Pay it off later.
   - *"Hold on to that."*
   - *"Remember them."*
7. **The roadmap.** One or two lines after the cold open, before the chronology.
   - *"Start in the station, with the two men who watched him board. Then the ride, hill by hill."*
8. **Clock-time chronology.** The event is told minute by minute with exact local times. *"At twenty-one minutes past eight, Saudi time…"*
9. **Explainers placed just before they matter.** A mechanism (a lock, a door, a control column, a sensor) is explained in 3–6 short lines immediately before the moment it fails or matters.
10. **The three ways.** Mechanism stories split the unknown into an enumerated set.
    - *"That leaves, broadly, three ways out. The bar never fully locked. It locked, but didn't hold him. Or something in it let go."*
    - Then take each in turn with *"One, …"*, *"Two, …"*, *"Three, …"*, each with a real precedent.
11. **The precedent header.** Open each comparison case with a place-date fragment.
    - *"Lakeside, Ohio, 2011."*
    - *"July 2013. A park in Texas."*
    - Then the case in 6–12 lines, ending with the official finding.
    - Then a firewall line: *"Texas is not Georgia. A different ride from a different maker."*
12. **The pivot question.** It opens the investigation half.
    - *"But one question is now bigger than all the others."*
    - *"So how does a rider come out of a seat built to hold him?"*
13. **Officialdom, quoted exactly.** Show how each authority characterises the event, in its own words, from the most cautious to the strongest.
    - *"The airline calls it an altercation. The regulator calls it a security incident. The minister goes much further."*
14. **The status wrap.** Close the factual part with a dated status block.
    - *"Here is where it stands on Thursday, the fifteenth of October."*
    - Then three parts: what is known, what isn't, and where the answers sit (which agencies, records or recordings hold them).
    - Include the presumption-of-innocence line when anyone is accused: *"Until a court decides, he is presumed innocent."*
15. **The quiet ending.** Return to a person, and finish on their own words or the opening image. No moral, no call to action.
    - *"You go to get the rush, she says. You always expect to get back."*

## 6. Ethics written into the voice
- **People's names.**
  - Name a deceased person only after officials or family have named them, and once is enough.
  - Never name minors, and never name a suspect whom no authority has named. Say so in the script: *"No authority has named him, and neither will we."*
- **Graphic detail.** There is no gore and no description of injuries beyond the officially stated parts.
  - Do not reconstruct a death itself in detail. Show the moment before, or the place, or an empty seat.
- **Family wishes.** If the family asks for something, honour it on screen and say so: *"His family asked people not to share videos of his final moments. None are in this film."*
- **Accusations.** Attribute every one. Separate the event (what happened) from the conclusion (why), and keep the "why" with the investigators unless officially established.
- **Sponsors and calls to action.** Keep them out of the story. If the user wants a sponsor read, place it at a natural break about a quarter of the way in, introduced by a single bridge line, and return with *"Now, back to the story."*

## 7. Structure (long-form)
| Part | Share | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open | 0:00–1:15 | Opener → witness → the event in 6–10 lines → mystery line |
| Litany + method | ~45 s | "This is the story of…" → central question → method line → (optional one-line CTA) → roadmap |
| Setting | 2–3 min | Date, place, the vehicle/ride/building explained, the people introduced with one fact each, plants |
| The event | 3–4 min | Minute-by-minute reconstruction; explainers just in time; quotes; stated unknowns |
| Aftermath | 2–3 min | Rescue, landing, statements, closures, the outside world's view |
| Investigation | 2–4 min | Pivot question → how officials characterise it → background → three ways / precedents |
| Status | 1–1.5 min | "Here is where it stands…" known / unknown / where the answers sit |
| Ending | 30–60 s | A person, their words, the opening image returned |

Short-form (60–90 s):
1. The opener (one line).
2. The event in 4–6 lines, with one quote.
3. The central question.
4. The single most important known fact and the single most important unknown.
5. A one-line status with the date.
6. Close on a quote. No litany and no method line; at most one source tag on screen.

## 8. Writing the visual into the script
The script carries light visual intent so the shot list can follow it. Put a note in square brackets on its own line before the paragraph it covers. Tools ignore these notes, and the voice never reads them.

```
[3D clay cockpit, slow push past the door; tag CONTROL COLUMN]
At the front of the plane is the flight deck. Two seats. Two control columns, one in front of each pilot, linked together.
```
- Every number that is spoken appears on screen in that shot: a stat card, a counter or a pinned label.
- Every time of day that is spoken gets a time chip.
- Every place gets a map label or a lower-left place caption.
- Every quote from an article gets that article on screen with the phrase highlighted as it is read.
- Every quote from a person gets a quote card, or a name lower-third over the reconstruction.
- Every stated unknown can get its own beat: hold the 3D, add the footnote "not published", or show an empty card.

## 9. Self-check before showing the script
Run `python tools/script_lint.py projects/<slug>/script/script_v01.md --format long|short` and fix every FAIL. Then reread it out loud in your head:
- Could a viewer point to the source of every factual sentence?
- Is anything stated as fact that is only one person's account?
- Is every quote verbatim, attributed and dated where it matters?
- Are all names allowed (section 6)?
- Does every chapter open with a concrete image, and does the ending land on a person's words?
