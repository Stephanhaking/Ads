## ACT 1: Hook (0:00 a 0:55)

[ON SCREEN: big "7:58."; porta fechada; a mão atravessa; "YOU CAN WALK THROUGH WALLS."]

It's seven fifty-eight. Your meeting starts at eight. The door to the building is locked.

You press your palm against it. And your hand goes in.

Not through a gap. Through the wood. Like the door was a glass of water.

You laugh. Then you stop laughing. You look around the street. Nobody saw. You try it on a lamppost. On a parked car. Every time, your hand goes in, and every time it comes back.

You pull your hand back. Your fingers are fine. You push again. Then you step forward, and the door is behind you.

Congratulations. You can walk through walls.

By tonight, you'll have learned three things about this power. The first two are problems. The third one is the reason you wouldn't want it.

Keep one word in your head. Touch.

---

## ACT 2: Why you can't do this today (0:55 a 2:40)

[ON SCREEN: átomo; "100,000×?"; sinal de "SIMPLIFICATION"; dois eletrões a repelirem-se; "PAULI."]

First question. Why can't you do this right now?

You've probably heard that atoms are mostly empty space. The nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom around it, depending on the element. So your hand should slide through a wall like one fog through another.

It doesn't. And the reason is that "mostly empty" is a simplification.

The space between the nucleus and the outside of the atom isn't nothing. It's filled with electrons, spread out as clouds, and with the forces between them. And those forces are what you actually touch.

Two things stop you. First, electrons carry a negative charge, and negative charges push each other away. The electrons in your hand push on the electrons in the wall. Second, a rule of quantum physics, called the Pauli exclusion principle, says two electrons can't sit in the same state. Squeeze atoms together, and they refuse.

Press your hand flat on a table right now. That feeling, that firm push back, is the electrons in the table pushing on the electrons in your skin.

That's what "solid" really means. Not matter bumping into matter. Forces saying no.

Is there a loophole? There is. It's called quantum tunneling. A single particle can sometimes cross a barrier it shouldn't be able to cross. It's real. In nineteen eighty-one, two researchers at IBM in Zurich used it to build a microscope that can see individual atoms. They won the Nobel Prize in Physics for it, in nineteen eighty-six.

But for a whole human body, the chance of tunneling through a wall is effectively zero.

So your power isn't physics. It's something extra. Fine. Let's ask a simpler question: what would your body need to do, to make it work?

---

## ACT 3: The first catch, one breath (2:40 a 4:30)

[ON SCREEN: big "CATCH #1."; parede por dentro, escura; relógio; "ONE BREATH."; DRAMATIZATION tag]

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined scene; you, in the first hour]

For the first hour, it's a joy. The back door of the office. The storage room. A shortcut through the neighbor's wall, because you can.

Then you do something small. You step into a wall, and you stop halfway to look around.

Dark. Completely dark. A wall is solid and opaque, and there is no light inside it. And no air, either.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined scene]

Your heart is pounding. You start to count. One. Two. Three. You push forward, your hand finds nothing, and then something cold: the other side. You step out into daylight, gasping. You counted to nineteen.

Most people can hold their breath for about thirty to ninety seconds. That's all you have. Not a day. Not an hour. One breath.

And here's the part nobody mentions. You can't see where the wall ends. You choose a direction in the dark, and you walk. A thin wall takes a few steps. A thick one, a stone one, takes much longer, and you can't tell which one you're in.

So the first catch is simple. Every wall is a bet, and the price is one breath.

---

## ACT 4: The second catch, the floor (4:30 a 6:25)

[ON SCREEN: big "CATCH #2."; chão; seta a descer; Terra em corte; contador "38 MIN."; DRAMATIZATION tag]

Now the second problem. And it's a strange one.

You walked through the door, and then you stood on the floor. Why didn't you fall?

Think about it. A floor is just a wall lying down. If your body ignored every wall, it would ignore the floor too. And gravity wouldn't care.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined scene]

You feel your feet sink. Through the carpet. Through the concrete. Through the basement. The ground doesn't stop you. You keep falling.

Here's what would happen, if nothing stopped you. If the Earth had the same density everywhere and no friction, it would take about forty-two minutes to fall all the way to the other side. With the real density of the Earth, which is higher in the core, physicists calculate about thirty-eight. And if nothing stopped you there either, you'd fall back. Again and again.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined scene]

You grab a railing and pull yourself back up. It works, because for that one railing you decided to be solid. Your power isn't a switch. It's a thousand tiny switches, one for every thing you touch.

That's the second catch. Your power can't be "ignore everything." It has to be selective. Wall: yes. Floor: no. Air: yes, because you need to breathe. Door handle: only when you decide.

Who makes that choice? Not physics. You. Every step. And a mistake costs you the ground.

---

## ACT 5: The third catch, touch (6:25 a 8:45)

[ON SCREEN: big "CATCH #3."; mão a segurar uma caneca; seta para "TOUCH."; neutrino a atravessar a pessoa; contador de neutrinos; DRAMATIZATION tag]

So we come back to the word. Touch.

Remember what stops you from walking through a wall. Electrons pushing on electrons. Now look at your day. That is the same force that holds a mug in your hand. It holds a phone, a keyboard, a door handle. It keeps your feet on the floor. It's why you can sit in a chair. It's even why you can digest dinner. Chemistry, the way atoms bind into molecules, is made of the same electromagnetic forces between electrons. The force that stops you at the wall is also part of what holds your body together.

Every time you touch something, you're saying: yes, push back.

Switch it off, and you don't just lose the wall. A body that ignored every interaction couldn't hold anything. It couldn't hear, because sound is matter moving. It couldn't see, because light would pass through your eyes without being absorbed.

There is something in the universe that behaves like that. Neutrinos. Tiny particles, made in huge numbers by the Sun. Estimates say tens of trillions of them pass through your body every second. Through you. Through the wall. Through the Earth.

They're the real wall-walkers. And what's it like to be one? Nothing ever happens. They almost never touch anything. They see nothing. They hold nothing.

That's the third catch. To walk through everything, you have to touch nothing. And you were just trying to get to a meeting.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined scene]

You stand in the street outside the locked door, switch the power off, and put your hand on the handle. It's cold. It pushes back. And you realize that was the best part of the day.

---

## ACT 6: Back to the door (8:45 a 10:10)

[ON SCREEN: big "7:58."; a porta; "TOUCH."; a mão a empurrar e a porta a resistir]

So let's go back to the door. Seven fifty-eight. Locked.

The real way to cross a wall exists. It happens one electron at a time, in a laboratory, and it lets us see atoms. That's the small, honest version of your power.

Everything else is a trade. Let's count what it costs. One breath, every time you cross a wall. A choice, with every step, between floor and wall. And the loss of everything that lets you hold anything at all.

A locked door is a promise someone made to someone else. You can break it without a sound. The power doesn't tell you whether you should.

You came in wanting to go through a wall. But the wall was never what kept you out. It's the same push that keeps you here. That keeps you standing. That lets you hold a mug, a hand, a door.

You push on the door. It holds.

Good. Remember the word. Touch.
