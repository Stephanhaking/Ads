## ACT 1: Hook (0:00 a 0:55)

[ON SCREEN: big "735 STORES." then count 30 000 JOBS; then count 400 000 000 (obj: coin) com legenda INTEREST. EVERY YEAR.]

In March of twenty eighteen, Toys R Us announced it was closing all seven hundred and thirty-five of its American stores. ⚠ About thirty thousand people lost their jobs. ⚠ Everyone blamed Amazon. It was the wrong suspect.

Here's the number that matters. Four hundred million dollars. That's roughly what Toys R Us paid in interest, every single year. ⚠ Not to open a store. Not to build a website. Just to carry a debt. A debt it never chose.

It was handed to the company the day three investors bought it. They borrowed the money to buy it, and then they made the toy store pay the loan back.

Keep that number in your head. And remember who got paid.

This is the story of a toy store that was bought with its own loan. And why the people who bought it were not the ones who paid.

## ACT 2: The giraffe's kingdom (0:55 a 2:20)

[ON SCREEN: Washington, D.C., 1948: obj calendar-circled-dates; "BABIES GROW UP."; toy-windmill; "ONE ROOF. EVERY TOY."; gravura de uma girafa (NÃO usar a mascote nem o logótipo da marca)]

To see what was lost, start small. Nineteen forty-eight. Washington, D.C. A young war veteran named Charles Lazarus opens a store that sells baby furniture. ⚠

It does fine. But he notices something. Babies grow up. A family buys one crib, and never comes back.

Toys were different. Kids broke them. Kids outgrew them. Kids wanted new ones. Parents came back again and again. ⚠

So in nineteen fifty-seven, Lazarus opens a store that sells only toys. ⚠ He gives it a name you can read from a highway, with a backward R, so it looks like a child wrote it. ⚠ Toys R Us.

Then he builds the whole toy aisle into one giant room. Low prices. High shelves. And a giraffe called Geoffrey as the mascot. ⚠ For a generation of kids, this was the place.

Remember the giraffe. We'll need him at the end.

## ACT 3: The wrong suspect (2:20 a 4:15)

[ON SCREEN: big "THE WRONG SUSPECT."; obj handshake; shipping-box; DRAMATIZATION tag; gavel; count 51 000 000]

Now, the story everyone tells. Amazon. And there really is an Amazon chapter. It's just not the one you think.

In the year two thousand, Toys R Us makes a deal with Amazon. Amazon will run the toy store's website, and Toys R Us will be the exclusive toy seller there, until twenty ten. ⚠ For a company built on stores, it sounds like a smart shortcut to the internet.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined meeting; roles, not names]
"Why build our own online store? Let Amazon do it."
"And if they let other toy sellers in?"
"They can't. It's in the contract."

Then Amazon lets other toy sellers in. ⚠ Toys R Us sues, in two thousand and four. In March of two thousand and six, a judge rules that Amazon broke the deal. ⚠ Toys R Us is free to walk away. And years later, Amazon pays Toys R Us fifty-one million dollars to settle. ⚠

So here's what nobody tells you. Toys R Us beat Amazon in court. It had spent six years with its online store in Amazon's hands. ⚠ A costly detour, probably. But companies survive detours.

Now think about what that tells you. If Amazon had been the killer, the story would end in two thousand and six, with a win. A company that has just beaten the biggest retailer on earth in court does not die of that retailer. It dies of something quieter.

Something that doesn't show up on a shelf, or in a headline, or in a lawsuit. Something that sits in a spreadsheet, and charges you every single day, whether the store is busy or empty.

What companies can't survive is a bill they can't pay. And that bill was already on its way.

## ACT 4: The buyout (4:15 a 6:15)

[ON SCREEN: big "2005."; house-keys-tag; signed-contract-pen; scale (equity vs debt: 1.3 vs 6.6); DRAMATIZATION tag; obj ledger]

Two thousand and five. Three investors want Toys R Us: Bain Capital, KKR, and a real estate firm called Vornado. They agree to pay about six point six billion dollars, plus the debt the company already carried. ⚠

Here's the trick, and it's perfectly legal. They don't use six billion of their own. They put in about one point three billion. ⚠ The rest, they borrow.

And here's the part that matters. The loan doesn't sit with them. It sits on the company they just bought.

Think of buying a house with a mortgage. Now imagine the house has to make the payments.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined boardroom; roles, not names]
"How do we pay for it?"
"The company pays."
"The company we're buying?"
"Its cash flow covers the interest. That's the model."

That's called a leveraged buyout. The buyers use the target's own future earnings as collateral for the money they borrow to buy it.

And to be fair, many of them work. If the company grows, the loan shrinks next to it, and the buyers walk away rich. But the math only works if the company grows faster than the interest does.

When it doesn't, the loan becomes a weight. And the weight doesn't fall on the buyers. The buyers put in a fraction. The company carries the rest.

Toys R Us wasn't growing faster. It was a big, old chain, fighting Walmart, Target, and a rising Amazon. ⚠

On the day that deal closed, Toys R Us didn't get a single new store, a new website, or a new idea. It got a new loan.

## ACT 5: The squeeze (6:15 a 8:00)

[ON SCREEN: count 400 000 000 a repetir ao lado de "NOT ONE NEW STORE."; broken-chain; price-chain; obj cart; barra de dívida a crescer; DRAMATIZATION tag]

Remember that four hundred million? ⚠ Every year, it came off the top. Before a new store. Before a remodel. Before a website that could compete.

Picture a family with a mortgage so big that, after paying it, there's nothing left for the roof. For years you patch the leaks. You say, next year. Then the storm comes.

Now run the thought experiment. Four hundred million dollars a year. Over a decade, that's four billion. ⚠ Not a forecast, just arithmetic. Four billion dollars you could have spent on new stores, better prices, a website that worked.

Instead, it went to the lenders. Every year. On schedule. Even in the years when sales were weak. Especially in those years, because the interest doesn't care about the sales.

That was Toys R Us. Critics say the stores aged while the money went to interest. ⚠ Analysts also point to rivals with cheaper prices and bigger budgets. ⚠ Both are probably true. But only one of those problems was chosen.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined meeting; roles, not names]
"The stores need work."
"With what money?"
"..."

By two thousand and seventeen, the debt stood at about five point two billion dollars. ⚠ And in September of that year, Toys R Us filed for bankruptcy. ⚠ A three-billion-dollar loan kept the stores open while it tried to fix things. ⚠

Bankruptcy is supposed to be a reset. A company gets breathing room, cuts what it can't afford, and tries again. But a reset needs two things: customers, and time. By then, Toys R Us had lost both.

It didn't work.

## ACT 6: Who got paid (8:00 a 9:50)

[ON SCREEN: big "WHO GOT PAID?"; count 735; count 30 000; count 470 000 000; count 20 000 000; split-coin; ledger]

In March of twenty eighteen, the company said it would close every American store. About thirty thousand workers. Most of them, with no severance. ⚠

Now remember the question from the beginning. Who got paid?

The three investors lost what they put in. That's true. When a company goes bankrupt, owners are last in line. ⚠ So it isn't that they won.

But look at the other side of the ledger. Bloomberg reported that, over the years, the three firms collected more than four hundred and seventy million dollars in fees and interest from Toys R Us. ⚠

Workers asked for severance. In the end, Bain and KKR set up a twenty-million-dollar fund. Vornado did not join. ⚠

Four hundred and seventy million, in fees and interest. Twenty million, in help. I'll leave you to decide whether that was fair.

[DRAMATIZATION: imagined cashier at closing time; role, not a name]
"Do we get anything?"
"There's a fund. It's small."
"And the people who owned it?"
"They lost their investment."

Both things are true. The owners lost what they put in. And they had been paid along the way. That's the part the headline never fits.

---

## ACT 7: The giraffe (9:50 a 10:45)

[ON SCREEN: a girafa em gravura (genérica); count 700+ STORES ABROAD; big "FOUR HUNDRED MILLION A YEAR."; "WHO PAYS THE INTEREST?"]

And the giraffe? He survived. When the company collapsed, the brand was sold, and Geoffrey went with it. ⚠ By twenty twenty-one, a new owner said it wanted to reopen stores in America, and more than seven hundred Toys R Us stores were still open around the world. ⚠ The name outlived the company.

So here's the lesson. Toys R Us didn't lose its customers to Amazon. It lost them, year after year, to four hundred million dollars.

The next time a company you love is bought with borrowed money, ask one question. Not who's buying it. Who's paying the interest?

Because the answer, almost always, is the company. And the people who work there.

Remember the giraffe. He's still standing. But the toy store that raised him, the one with the high shelves and the backward R, was never beaten by a rival. It was bought. With its own loan.

One number. Four hundred million. Every year.
