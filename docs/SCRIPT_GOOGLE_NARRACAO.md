# GOOGLE — "The Company That Predicts Your Death"

Texto de locução (EN). Sem descrição de visuais nem beats.

## COLD OPEN

There's a moment in every hospital that nurses are trained to catch. The moment a patient starts to die. It hides in a hundred small readings, and it's easy to miss. In twenty eighteen, a machine learned to catch it first. Google took the medical records of a hundred and fourteen thousand people and fed them to an artificial intelligence. Then it asked one question. Who, in this building, is going to die? It answered with ninety-five per cent accuracy. Earlier than the charts. Earlier than the nurses. Sometimes, earlier than the doctor standing at the bed. The result was published in a medical journal. And it leaves one strange question hanging. Why is an advertising company this good at knowing when you'll die?

## ACT I

At first, this looks like a story about Google moving into healthcare. But the hospital is almost beside the point. Look at what the machine had to do. To see a death coming, it had to be very good at one narrow thing. Take everything known about a person, and work out what happens next. And that's the oldest skill Google has. It's spent twenty years perfecting it on the rest of us. Same engine. Pointed at the final variable.

## ACT II

So how does a company get good enough to forecast a death? It reads all of it. The numbers. The scans. Even the free-text notes a nurse types at three in the morning. Forty-six billion data points, all feeding one set of predictions. And the raw material arrived the way it usually does. A hospital handed Google the records of every patient who came through its doors over seven years, and called them anonymous. But the dates were still attached. So were the doctors' notes. And for a company that already knows where you were on a given afternoon, a dated, annotated medical file doesn't stay anonymous for long. The patients were never asked. No one had to ask them.

## ACT III

A prediction engine this powerful is also expensive, and it has to earn its keep. To see how, look at what Google actually spent those twenty years building: a system that turns human behaviour into something you can measure, store, and sell. Every search is a measurement. Every route, every video, every pause. On their own, they are just readings. Point them at a market, though, and each reading becomes a bet — on what a person will do next, sold in a fraction of a second to whoever wants that person's next decision. Last year, Alphabet took in four hundred and three billion dollars. Roughly three of every four came from that one market. The death algorithm shows how good the engine has become. Advertising is where the engine gets paid.

## ACT IV

And here the story turns, because a prediction has a dangerous property. It does not have to stay a prediction. If you know what someone will probably do, you can start adjusting the world around them until probably quietly becomes certainly. A default set here. A feed arranged there. A nudge so small no one feels it. Accuracy is worth pennies; certainty is worth a fortune — and the cheapest way to buy certainty is to shape the behaviour you have already sold. So the machine drifts, without anyone deciding it should, from watching to steering. Predict. Nudge. Confirm the nudge worked. Predict again, a little more sure each time. Nobody chose this. The incentive chose it.

## ACT V

You don't have to imagine what steering looks like. It has already happened, in the open, on a product you use every day. Around twenty sixteen, YouTube quietly changed the question its algorithm was trying to answer. Not what does this person want to watch — but what will keep this person watching the longest. It tuned itself to chase a single number: watch time. And it worked. By the company's own account, seventy per cent of everything watched on YouTube now comes from what the machine plays next, not from what anyone searched for. A billion hours a day. The average session on a phone runs past an hour. And most of it was chosen for you, by a system that learned one simple lesson — the surest way to keep someone watching is to hand them the next video before they decide to leave. Nobody is forcing you to stay. You are simply being pointed, gently, precisely, a thousand times a day, by a machine optimising for a number, on a screen you opened yourself.

And if that still sounds like reading too much into a recommendation, consider that Google's own designers reached the same conclusion, years ago, and wrote it down. In twenty sixteen, a team inside the company made an internal film. It described your accumulated data as a ledger — a record that could be passed on, and outlive you — and it imagined using that ledger to nudge people toward chosen goals: goals that, in its own words, reflect Google's values as an organisation. Not one user. Whole populations. When the film leaked to the press, Google called it a thought experiment, a provocation, nothing more. And perhaps it was. But you do not sit down to imagine steering human behaviour at scale unless you are already holding the machine that could. It was never a fantasy about the future. It was a description of the engine they had already built.

## ACT VI

And the machine has a great many hands on the wheel, because you fitted most of them yourself. The search box hears what you want. The browser, on roughly seven in ten screens, sees where you go. The phone, on seven in ten handsets on Earth, knows where you are. The map keeps the history. You downloaded all of it, signed in, and said thank you. Where Google does not own the window, it rents it — paying Apple around twenty billion dollars a year to stay the default on a phone it never made. In twenty twenty-four, after years of this, a court reached for the plainest word it had, and used it: monopoly. Illegal. A company that had already won, spending its days defending the win.

## ACT VII

So why does a study from twenty eighteen matter more now than it did then? Because the machine just grew a new mouth. The largest record of human behaviour ever gathered is exactly what you need to build an artificial intelligence — and exactly what you need to make it answer you. The company that learned to predict your death is now the one you turn to when you have a question. When a machine built to predict your next click can predict your last breath, and then becomes the thing you ask about everything in between, it has stopped watching the window from outside. It has quietly become the glass you look through.

## ACT VIII

So here is the company, without the friendly colours. It gave you tools worth paying for and charged you nothing, because the fee was never money. It listens from windows you installed and thanked it for. It sells the future version of you to people you will never meet. And it has grown so good at forecasting human beings that it can tell a hospital you have a day left to live before the doctor at your bedside knows.

None of this needed a villain. It needed an incentive, pointed patiently in one direction for twenty years. You did not lose your privacy in a breach. You handed it over, one free click at a time, for a better map. The map was real. The price was real. You just never saw the meter.

The most valuable thing this company ever learned was never what you would buy. It was what you would do next.

Including the last thing.

This was Distinguish.
