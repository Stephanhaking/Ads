## Cold open

A thousand years ago, there was a moment when a peasant made the most important choice of his life. It wasn't what to plant. It was who to hand his freedom to.

Today, you make that same choice dozens of times a day. Except nobody asks you to raise your hand. You just tap "Accept."

This is a very old story. And it will show you that today's story was already written.

## Act I — Before the castles

In the ninth century, Europe was under attack from every side. Vikings from the north. Magyars from the east. Raiders from the south. Kings were far away, and their armies were farther.

If you lived in the countryside, you figured out one simple thing very quickly: nobody was coming to defend you. You found a protector, or you didn't survive.

And the nearest protector was always the same man. The strongman of the region. The one with horses, weapons, and somewhere to retreat. First a wooden tower on a hill. Later, a fortress of stone.

Notice the logic. Nobody built the castle to rob you. They built it to protect you. The robbing came later, slowly, once the protection was already accepted.

## Act II — The contract

When a peasant accepted a lord, there was even a ceremony. He knelt, placed his hands between the lord's hands, and swore loyalty. We recognize the image today, because it looks strangely like a handshake before signing a paper.

The deal was simple. The lord guaranteed protection and let the peasant work a piece of land. In return, the peasant gave three things.

First, labor. In many cases, several days a week working the lord's own fields, before he could tend his own.

Second, a share of the harvest. And a tenth more went to the Church.

Third, and the most important one: the freedom to leave. Serfs were tied to the land. The land belonged to the lord, and the serfs came with it.

In many places, even the most personal moments had a price. Marrying off a daughter could mean paying a fee to the lord. **[VERIFY: examples and regional variations, e.g. "merchet" in England]**

And notice something. Nobody held a knife to their throats the day they agreed. It was a trade. They were afraid, and the lord had a solution. Only later did they understand the price.

## Act III — The door and the toll

There was one brilliant detail in this system. The lord owned the mill, the oven, and the wine press.

If you wanted to grind your wheat, you had to use the lord's mill, and pay. If you wanted to bake your bread, the oven was his. And owning your own was forbidden.

See the trick? The lord didn't live by threatening you every day. He lived by being the point everything had to pass through. Every time you needed something basic, there was a door, and next to the door, someone with an open hand.

## Act IV — The new fief

Now think about your phone.

When you accept the terms of a social network, you hand over data about who you are, where you are, and what you want. You hand over your attention, hours a day. In return you get access: to your friends, your work, your photos, the map that takes you home.

Just like the peasant, nobody held a knife to your throat. There was convenience. You had a problem, and someone had a free solution.

And just like the peasant, only later do we start to understand the price. Your data stops being yours. And it isn't only your privacy: it's a company knowing more about you than you know yourself, and using that to predict you.

## Act IV-B — A day in the life of a digital serf

He wakes up to his phone's alarm. Before getting out of bed, he has already opened three apps. One tells him what happened while he slept. Another shows him the weather. The third shows him the faces of people he knows, doing things he didn't do.

He heads to work, and the map knows the way. He asks the search engine a question. At lunch, he buys something online. In the evening, he watches a video, then another, then another, because the next one is already starting by itself.

None of these moments had a visible price. And yet, by the end of the day, several companies know where he went, what he searched for, what he almost bought, and how long he hesitated.

Each gesture is small. It's the sum that makes the portrait. And that portrait is worth money, because it lets them predict what he will do next.

## Act V — The rent

But the strongest parallel isn't the data. It's the rent.

Think about the app store on your phone. If you build an app and want people to download it, you have to go through that store. And under the usual rules, the platform takes a commission of up to thirty percent of what you earn. **[VERIFY: current percentages and exceptions]**

Think about someone selling on a big online marketplace. The seller pays to be there, pays to appear first, pays for logistics. **[VERIFY: total fee percentage, independent source]**

It's the mill all over again. Nobody needs to forbid you from grinding wheat. All they need is to own the only mill in the region.

## Act V-B — Two real cases

These aren't examples invented for the video. They have already reached courtrooms.

In 2020, Epic Games, the company behind a hugely popular game, sued Apple over the rules and commissions of its app store. Epic argued it couldn't sell directly to players, or even tell them that a cheaper price existed outside the store. **[VERIFY: dates, claims and rulings; the court sided with Apple on most points but limited the blocking of external links]**

And in the United States, the competition authority sued Amazon, alleging that the company penalized sellers who offered lower prices elsewhere, and that the total fees had become hard to avoid. **[VERIFY: filing date, exact allegations and current status]**

Notice the pattern. In both cases, the complaint isn't only the price. It's the door. Whoever controls the passage decides the rules, and whoever wants to reach the customers has to accept.

## Act V-C — The contract that changes by itself

There's one more difference that makes all of this subtler. The peasant's contract stayed written, or at least known to everyone. Yours doesn't.

A platform's terms change. Often without a clear warning, sometimes with a long text almost nobody reads, and a button at the bottom. You accept, because the alternative is losing access.

They tell you it's your choice. But choosing between accepting everything or losing your digital life isn't really a choice. It's the same dilemma the peasant faced before the lord: a little more protection, a little less freedom, every time.

And what's asked for today isn't only labor or harvest. It's knowledge about you.

## Act V-D — When the lord knows you

In the first video on this channel, we told how a company trained a system to predict, from hospital records, who was at risk of dying. Think about what that means: a machine able to look at your data and tell what's going to happen to you.

Now think of the same principle, applied to what you buy, what you watch, what you feel. The medieval lord knew your fields. Today's lord knows your habits. And whoever knows your habits can influence your choices, almost without you noticing.

## Act VI — Who said it first

This idea isn't mine. The Greek economist Yanis Varoufakis calls it technofeudalism, in the 2023 book with that name. For him, the big platforms no longer compete in a market: they own the territory where the market happens, and they charge rent for you being there. Before him, the French economist Cédric Durand had written something similar, in *Techno-féodalisme*, in 2020. **[VERIFY: titles, years and each author's thesis]**

## Act VII — The limits of the comparison

But let's be honest. The comparison has limits, and some economists disagree with it. **[VERIFY: cite a specific critic, e.g. Evgeny Morozov's critique of the concept]**

Some say this isn't feudalism, it's capitalism with monopolies. The companies still compete with each other, there are laws trying to rein them in, and there are alternatives.

The peasant couldn't change lords. You can delete an account.

But deleting an account is one thing. Losing ten years of photos, contact with your friends, your job or your customers is another. That's what makes leaving expensive. It isn't forbidden. It's just very costly. And for today's lords, that cost is the equivalent of the walls.

You can agree with the analogy or not. What matters is what it lets you see: where the power is, who charges you to pass, and what you lose if you leave.

## Act VIII — How feudalism ended

So how did feudalism end? Not with a battle.

In the middle of the fourteenth century, the Black Death killed a huge part of Europe's population. **[VERIFY: mortality estimates, usually cited as between a third and a half in many regions]** Suddenly, workers were scarce. And a peasant who knows the lord needs him has, for the first time, something to negotiate with. He could ask for more, go somewhere else, or move to the city.

Cities grew. Trade, money, and crafts offered another way of living, far from the lord's mill. There was even a German saying: "city air makes you free." **[VERIFY: origin and usage of "Stadtluft macht frei"]**

Notice what changed. It wasn't the lords becoming kind. It was alternatives appearing, and people with the power to choose them.

## Close

Feudalism teaches one thing well. It ended when people found another path, when the rules changed, and when they realized the deal could be different.

You don't need to delete everything and live in a cabin. You need to know what you're handing over, to whom, and in exchange for what. And to know that the exit exists, even when it's expensive.

In the next video, we'll see, step by step, how to leave the castle without leaving the world.
