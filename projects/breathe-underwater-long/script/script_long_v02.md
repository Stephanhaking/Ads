## Cold open

[3D clay: a lake at dusk, a pier, a swimmer slips off the end; camera follows under; time stack "Day one · 2 m down"; badge SIMULATION]
Two metres under a lake. A pier above, a girl in a red T-shirt below.
One person. Eyes open. Mouth open.
In the corner of the frame, a stopwatch starts.
Ten seconds. Thirty. A minute.
She is not holding her breath. She is breathing.
The water goes in. Something comes out. Her chest rises and falls like anyone's on dry land.
Nobody on the pier knows how. The answer is not in her lungs. It is in a number.
Two hundred and fifty.

## The story of

[Fast cuts: jar of air, jar of water, a stack of buckets, a gill, a mouse in a tank, an empty pier]
This is the story of one rule. A person who can breathe water.
Of a litre of air and a litre of lake. And the gap between them.
Of a fish that solves the problem with a plumbing trick. And a lung that was never built for it.
Of mice in a tank in the nineteen-sixties, and premature babies in a hospital in Philadelphia.
And an empty pier at dusk.
Could a human really do it? And what would it cost her, minute by minute?
This film is built on physiology textbooks, diving manuals, clinical reviews and old laboratory reports. It is a calculation, not a prediction. And the unknowns are many. Every number comes from a named source, or from arithmetic we show. Where the sources disagree, we say so.
Start with the number. Then the water. Then the fish. Then the liquid.

## Setting

[3D clay: cross-section of a torso, lungs highlighted; stat "250 mL / minute"; footnote "resting adult · physiology course data"]
First, what a body needs.
At rest, an adult uses about two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen a minute. That is the figure in standard physiology teaching. One medical exam-prep page gets there from three and a half millilitres per kilogram, for a seventy-kilogram adult. About two hundred and forty-five.
Two hundred and fifty. Hold on to that.
In air, it is easy to supply. A litre of dry air is about twenty point nine percent oxygen, according to the United Nations food agency's fisheries manual. That is about two hundred and nine millilitres in every litre.
Resting breathing moves about five to eight litres of air a minute, according to the standard reference on minute ventilation. Call it six.
Six litres of air. Two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen. A comfortable, quiet job.
[3D: the swimmer, calm, a gauge showing 6 L/min]
That is the deal the lungs were built around.
Now change the fluid.

## The first breath

[Split: two jars on a clay table; left 209 mL, right 6 mL; time chip "25 °C"]
Water holds oxygen too. Not much, says the sensor maker.
A manufacturer of dissolved-oxygen sensors says air-saturated water at twenty-five degrees holds about eight point three milligrams of oxygen per litre.
Convert that, and it is about five point eight millilitres. Our conversion, using a standard value for oxygen's density.
Two hundred and nine in air. Five point eight in water. About thirty-six times less.
Colder water holds more. Warm water holds less. But the order of magnitude stays.
[Counter climbs: 43 L]
So how much water does she need?
If her lungs took every last molecule, two hundred and fifty divided by five point eight is forty-three litres a minute.
That is the floor. Physics will not let it go lower.
But lungs do not take every molecule.
An arXiv paper on lung ventilation in mammals reports that exhaled air is sixteen to eighteen percent oxygen, when the air going in is twenty-one. A drop of three to five points.
That is roughly a seventh to a quarter of what comes in. Our arithmetic.
Take the generous end. A quarter.
[Counter: 172 L; bucket stack; tag "LUNGS TAKE A QUARTER · ASSUMPTION"]
Two hundred and fifty, divided by five point eight, divided by a quarter. One hundred and seventy-two litres a minute.
At the stingy end, it is more than three hundred.
[Chart: bar 172 L vs bar 140 L labelled "measured maximum"]
How much can a person breathe?
A physiology module at Monash University gives a mean maximum voluntary ventilation of about a hundred and forty litres a minute for healthy young men. Range, a hundred to a hundred and eighty. For women, about a hundred. Seventy to a hundred and twenty.
That is a short test, the module says, with the subject trying as hard as possible.
Her resting need is higher than that.
At rest.

## The weight of water

[3D clay: a crate on a scale reading 172 kg; tag "WATER ≈ 800× DENSER THAN AIR"; footnote "density ratio from physiology course notes"]
Then there is what water is.
It is about eight hundred times denser than air. Course notes give a ratio of about eight hundred and thirty, a flashcard set says.
One litre of water weighs one kilogram.
A hundred and seventy-two litres a minute is a hundred and seventy-two kilograms. Through the throat. Every minute. While resting.
[3D: a lung drawn as a tree; one breath enters; the tip fills]
And the lung has a second problem. It breathes in and out through the same pipes.
A textbook puts the anatomical dead space at about a hundred and fifty millilitres, out of a resting breath of five hundred.
That is the volume of the airways themselves. Whatever sits there at the end of an outbreath is stale. The next breath mixes with it.
In air, that costs a little. The textbook says it leaves the oxygen pressure in the lung lower than in the air outside.
In water, count it.
Five hundred millilitres in. A hundred and fifty of it stale. Three hundred and fifty reach the air sacs.
To move a hundred and seventy-two litres of fresh water through them, that is nearly five hundred breaths a minute.
[Counter: 491 breaths/min]
Or take the biggest breath she has. Total lung capacity is about six litres, and the usual vital capacity is four point six to four point eight, depending on the source.
A full breath every time. Nearly forty a minute. Each one about four and a half kilograms of lake. Our arithmetic.
Neither is a pace a person holds. No source we read says anyone has.

## What happens today

[3D clay: a swimmer goes limp; time chip; footnote "clinical review · not a reconstruction of a real case"]
All this is hypothetical. Here is what happens in the real world.
When someone inhales water, a clinical update on drowning describes two paths.
In roughly ten to twenty percent of victims, the throat clamps shut. Laryngospasm. The airway closes and the person asphyxiates, with little water reaching the lungs. Another source puts it lower, under ten to fifteen.
In most, the throat relaxes and water goes in.
Victims typically aspirate about two to four millilitres per kilogram, according to the same reviews. A small volume.
But it does damage. The lungs line their air sacs with a thin film called surfactant. It keeps them open. Water disrupts it. The sacs collapse. Oxygen can no longer cross.
Older teaching said fresh water and salt water injure the lungs differently. Current clinical sources say the practical difference is minor.
The result is the same chain. Collapse, falling oxygen, rising carbon dioxide, and then the heart.
So our hero's lungs would need two things. A way to avoid the damage. And a way to move far more water than any lung has moved.
Remember the second.

## The fish

[3D clay: a trout in a clear tank; gill filaments in cutaway; arrows showing flow in opposite directions; tag "COUNTERCURRENT"]
Fish do it. Every minute. How?
Two tricks.
One. Their gills run blood and water in opposite directions. The most oxygen-rich blood meets the most oxygen-rich water. The least meets the least. Diffusion keeps working along the whole surface.
How well does it work? Sources differ. A textbook says up to eighty-five percent of the oxygen in the water. A school resource says about eighty. The Natural History Museum in London says up to seventy. An animal-physiology text says ninety or more is possible.
Take eighty.
[Counter: 54 L/min]
Two hundred and fifty, divided by five point eight, divided by point eight. About fifty-four litres a minute. Not one hundred and seventy-two.
Two. The water flows one way. It goes in the mouth, over the gills, out the side. No dead space. No stale pool in the airways.
A physiology text says fish greatly reduce the energy cost of moving water by keeping that flow continuous and in one direction.
[Split: tidal lung vs one-way gill]
A lung does the opposite. It is a dead-end sac. The same pipe in, the same pipe out.
So there is a first way to make her breathing work.
Change her.
Give her gills. Or something with gills' efficiency. A one-way flow. A far better extraction.
That is a different animal. Nothing in this film says it is impossible. It is not a lung anymore. Whether any human body could be rebuilt that way is not known.

## The second way: change the liquid

[3D: a laboratory, nineteen-sixties; a sealed chamber; footnote "history compiled from secondary sources; originals not read"]
Duke University, early nineteen-sixties.
According to a Gizmodo history, Johannes Kylstra's team trained mice to breathe saline loaded with oxygen under a pressure of a hundred and sixty atmospheres.
The mice died minutes later. Carbon dioxide had built up.
Another account says rodents and even dogs lasted up to about an hour. Accounts differ on the time. They agree on the cause.
The saline delivered enough oxygen. It did not clear the carbon dioxide fast enough.
[Chart: oxygen in, carbon dioxide stuck]
The same fisheries manual says carbon dioxide is far more soluble in water than oxygen is. Which is one reason gills shed it easily.
But a saline-filled lung is not a gill. Our reading: it is the same dead-end sac, filled with a liquid that barely moves. The sources do not test that.
[3D: a mouse tank, bubbles; tag "PERFLUOROCARBON"]
Mid-nineteen-sixties. Clark and Gollan.
They switched the liquid. Not water. A perfluorocarbon. A fluid that dissolves both gases.
Gizmodo says their mice breathed it at normal pressure. Rats stayed under for up to twenty hours. Cats, for weeks.
An older encyclopedia mirror adds one detail that matters. The colder the liquid, the lower the animal's breathing rate.
That is the third way, and we will come back to it.
First, the cost.
Perfluorocarbons are denser and more viscous than air. That raises the effort of breathing, according to Wikipedia's entry on liquid breathing.
[Statement wall]
Mice are not people.
A mouse's lung is tiny. The distance is short. The volume is small. The sources here do not say whether the same method works at the size of an adult. That has not been shown.

## The third way: lower the demand

[3D clay: the swimmer, slow, a thermometer dropping; badge SIMULATION]
Look at what the cold did for the animals.
It lowered their breathing rate. That is a fall in demand.
Demand is the other half of the sum. If the body needed less oxygen, the two hundred and fifty would fall, and so would the litres.
For a human, the cold is not kind.
A boating-safety guide says water draws heat from the body about twenty-five times faster than air at the same temperature. Other sources give four, twenty, and thirty-two. They all say faster.
The first danger is not hypothermia. It is cold shock. Sudden immersion makes people gasp. Another guide says water inhaled in that gasp can cause unconsciousness within minutes.
Hands and legs go numb. Within minutes, according to a New York state boating guide.
Hypothermia comes later. One expert average is about an hour, depending on temperature, clothing and body type.
So the cold that helped the rats would not help her. Or not for long. Whether a human could be chilled safely enough to cut demand, while breathing liquid, has not been shown in the sources we found.

## The human case

[3D clay: a hospital incubator; footnote "premature neonates · partial liquid ventilation"]
Philadelphia, nineteen eighty-nine.
Three very premature babies. Twenty-eight, twenty-four and twenty-three weeks of gestation. Doctors at Jefferson Medical College, led by Dr. J. S. Greenspan, tried perfluorocarbon in their lungs.
A review reports that their lung compliance improved. The paper appeared in the Journal of Pediatrics in nineteen ninety.
Note what it was. Partial liquid ventilation. The lungs were filled with the liquid, and a ventilator still pushed gas through it. Not a baby breathing liquid alone.
[Chart: nineteen ninety-six bars: oxygen tension +138%, compliance +61%]
Nineteen ninety-six. The LiquiVent study group reported thirteen infants with severe respiratory distress. In the New England Journal of Medicine, they say that within an hour of the treatment, arterial oxygen tension rose by a hundred and thirty-eight percent. Compliance rose by sixty-one. No adverse events were clearly attributable to it.
That is the best result in this film.
Then the footnote. Other clinical trials, a review notes, failed to show that partial liquid ventilation reduced illness in acute respiratory failure.
It is not a lake, and it is not an adult swimmer. A premature lung is not a healthy one, and a controlled ward is not open water.
Whether a healthy adult could breathe liquid for hours has not been established.

## Down

[3D: the swimmer descends; a depth gauge counts; pressure labels at 10, 20, 30 m]
Now take her down.
Pressure rises about one atmosphere for every ten metres of seawater. That figure is consistent across diving sources.
At thirty metres, four atmospheres in total. Our sum.
Divers on air meet two problems there. One is the gas itself. A training article says the effects of inert-gas narcosis are second only to oxygen toxicity in hazard for an air diver.
The other is the bends. The extra pressure drives more nitrogen into the blood. Come up too fast and it comes out as bubbles. Joint pain. In severe cases, stroke or paralysis. The standard advice is an ascent of about ten metres a minute or slower.
Oxygen is the third. Dive rules cap its pressure. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's regional dive plan says nitrox diving is generally limited to one point four atmospheres. A quoted NOAA manual says one point six.
Air is twenty-one percent oxygen. Multiply by the pressure. About sixty metres on air, and the limit is reached. Our arithmetic, at four atmospheres per thirty.
But she is not breathing air.
What would pressure do to someone breathing water? Would nitrogen dissolve into her blood? Would it bubble on the way up?
That has not been published, as far as we could find. It is unclear what the answer would be.
