## Cold open (0:00)
[8:52 da manhã; um elevador de escritório; um boneco de fato e gravata amarela; um relógio de pulso; o relógio de parede a marcar 5:00]
You've woken up as Kento Nanami, from Jujutsu Kaisen. In real life.
You have one rule. Clock out at five, or die.
[CANON] In the story, he can find the weak point in almost anything, at the seven-to-three line.
[OUR RULE] In your version, a curse has moved into your office building. At exactly five p.m., the doors seal. Anyone still inside doesn't leave.
It's eight fifty-two in the morning. Your tie is on. You have eight hours.

## 8:52 a.m.: the line (0:35)
[o elevador; uma linha dourada a dividir uma porta em 7 e 3; uma fechadura a abrir-se; uma arma sem fio embrulhada em jornal]
[CANON] The Ratio Technique splits a target into ten parts. A strike at the seven-three point creates a weak spot, and the hit lands as a critical one.
The elevator doors are in front of you. You look at them, and a thin gold line appears, three tenths from the top.
You tap it with a knuckle. The whole door sighs, and the lock pops open as if it had been waiting.
In the coat closet by the entrance, wrapped in newspaper, there's a long, heavy, dull blade.
[CANON] In the story, his weapon is a blunt sword. It can still cut weaker things, because of the technique.
You weigh it in one hand. It feels like a very serious ruler.
[OUR RULE] In your version, you keep your own face and your own name tag. Only the habits are his. The calm. The coat. The way you check the time before you check anything else.
On the way to your floor, you test it once more on the coffee machine in the lobby. You tap the line. The machine gives you a perfect espresso and then, quietly, a second one, for free.
Nobody sees. Nobody ever sees. That's the other thing about being him: you become very good at being a man in a suit that no one looks at twice.
At eight fifty-nine, you sit at your desk with a blade under it, and a spreadsheet open on the screen.

## 10:00 a.m.: the schedule (2:20)
[uma reunião; um chefe de gravata; relógios por todo o lado; um temporizador de 5:00 PM no ecrã]
[CANON] Nanami's power is bound by a vow. During working hours, it's limited. After hours, it climbs.
[OUR RULE] In your version, until five p.m., you only get a working-hours version of the technique. Enough to see lines. Not enough to win a fight.
Your manager, Mr. Hale, leans over your cubicle with a folder and a smile.
"We'll need this report by six."
"I leave at five."
Everyone in the open office goes quiet. Somebody drops a stapler.
"Nobody leaves at five here," Mr. Hale says, as if reading it off a plaque. "That's how we got the Henderson account."
"That's how you lost four interns."
You don't mean to say it out loud. You say it out loud.
"Everyone leaves at five," you add. "I just mean it."
[CANON] In the story, Nanami left sorcery for an ordinary office job, and came back later.
You never thought you'd understand why he did that. Now you do, a little. Offices are a kind of curse too.
But the one in the stairwell isn't metaphorical.

## 12:30 p.m.: the stairwell (3:35)
[uma escadaria de emergência; uma pulsação a vermelho nas paredes; funcionários a dormir nas secretárias]
You feel it at lunch. A slow, wet pulse behind the fire door on the ninth floor.
Your technique shows you the building the way a doctor would. Lines everywhere. Some are clean. Some are cracked.
This one is knotted. Like a throat.
[OUR RULE] In your version, it's a curse that feeds on exhaustion. The longer people stay, the heavier it gets. It sealed itself in the building last night, and it wakes up every evening at five.
You look at the office. Forty people. Half of them are asleep at their desks with their eyes open.
Priya, from accounting, catches you staring. She has three monitors and a dead plant.
"You look like you've seen a ghost."
"I've seen a deadline."
"Well, it's mine. I'm staying until it's done."
You tell her to leave at five. She tells you to mind your own business. You both mean it.
"Are you okay?" she asks. "You've been looking at the walls all morning like you're measuring them."
"Seven to three," you say.
"What?"
"Nothing. It's a ratio. It's a work thing."
She looks at you for a long second. Then she goes back to her three monitors, and you go back to counting the minutes.

## 3:15 p.m.: Collapse (4:50)
[uma sala de arquivos; uma parede com uma linha 7:3; poeira; um teto a abanar]
You need a way out. For everyone. Fast.
[CANON] There's a second part of his technique, called Collapse. It applies the seven-three line to the environment itself: a wall, a floor. And the whole place crumbles from one strike.
You test it in the old file room, on a wall nobody will miss.
A single tap. The wall folds like wet paper.
The ceiling above it drops three inches and stops. Then a little more.
[OUR RULE] In your version, a wrong line doesn't open a door. It opens the floor. And if you don't know where the line is, the technique won't guess for you.
Lesson one: a weak point isn't a trick. It's a responsibility.
You mark the stairwell's glass wall with chalk, seven-three, and go back to your desk.
It's three forty-one. Mr. Hale wants to know why you're covered in dust.

## 4:40 p.m.: everybody out (6:10)
[o escritório a esvaziar-se; pessoas a correr; o relógio de ponto na entrada; 4:58]
At four forty, you pull the fire alarm.
It's the least cursed-energy thing you've ever done, and it works better than anything else could have.
Thirty-eight people file out, complaining, holding their coats, pointing at their phones. Somebody asks if it's a drill. Somebody asks if the cake is still happening.
Mr. Hale is still talking about the report.
You walk to the glass wall. One tap, at the chalk line.
The glass breaks open into a clean arch, wide enough for four people at a time.
At four fifty-eight, you're at the time clock. You tap your card. The machine says, in a small green font: four fifty-eight p.m. Clocked out.
You turn around.
Priya's still at her desk.
You walk back through the empty floor. Every desk lamp is still on. Every chair is still warm.
"I'm almost done," she says, without looking up.
"The building's going to close."
"It's a building. It doesn't close. It just... asks for more."
You almost laugh. It's the truest thing anyone's said all day.
It's four fifty-nine and fifty seconds.

## 5:00 p.m.: overtime (7:15)
[portas a selar-se com uma luz vermelha; o relógio da parede às 5:00; o relógio de pulso a brilhar]
The doors seal with a sound like a very large mouth closing.
You clock back in. You don't have a choice anymore.
[CANON] In the story, once overtime starts, his power jumps. A lot.
[OUR RULE] In your version, it's a loan. You get the power now. And a clock starts on your heart. Eleven minutes. If you're not clocked out by then, it stops.
Everything gets quieter. Everything gets sharper.
You see the lines on the curse before it even shows up. It's made of desks and cables and a thousand tiny clocks that all say five o'clock.
It rises out of the stairwell, as tall as the ceiling.
Priya says one word, quietly. It's not a word you can repeat.
You push her behind the cabinets and step forward.

## 5:06 p.m.: the line again (8:05)
[o monstro de secretárias e relógios; uma linha dourada no corpo; o boneco a acertar com a arma sem fio]
Five minutes left.
The curse hits first. You go through a wall. You feel a rib, or something near a rib, say no.
Your technique keeps the lines steady, though. Three tenths from the top. Three tenths from the top. Always three tenths.
[OUR GUESS] Our guess is that a fight like this isn't about strength. It's about not panicking when the clock is louder than the monster.
You swing the dull blade at the line on its chest. It rings like a bell, a bad bell, and your wrist goes white to the elbow.
Nothing. The curse laughs, with a hundred clock voices.
You aren't aiming at its chest. You're aiming at the place it hides its weakness: the old stairwell, where it came from.
Collapse. One tap. At the line, on the floor beneath it.
The ninth floor folds, and the curse folds with it. The desks slide. The clocks go off one after another, a hundred little alarms, each one ringing five.
You don't hear them. You hear only your own pulse, counting down.

## 5:11 p.m.: clock out (9:00)
[o relógio de ponto; um dedo a tocar um cartão; 5:11; um coração a bater de novo]
The doors unseal with a soft click.
Your heart is a metronome with something wrong in it. Thump. Thump. Then a long, long gap.
You crawl to the time clock. Priya's behind you, holding your tie like a leash.
You tap the card.
Five eleven p.m. Clocked out.
Your heart starts again, rude and loud.
The next morning, the building has a new notice by the door. A line for who left on time.
Priya brings you a coffee. It's bad. It's perfect.
"You knew," she says. "From the start. Didn't you?"
"I only knew one thing. That nothing in that building was worth being late for."
She thinks about that, and then she writes something on a sticky note and presses it onto your monitor. It says: GO HOME.
You leave at five. Not five oh one. Five.
Would you clock out on time, if the other option was a hundred more hours? Tell me in the comments. And if you want the next one, tell me who you want to become.
