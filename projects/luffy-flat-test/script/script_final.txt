You miss the last step of the subway stairs. You're falling backwards into a crowd of strangers. And then your arm stretches four metres across the station, wraps around a railing, and pulls you back like a bungee cord. Nobody saw it. Everybody was looking at their phone. You look at your hand. It's normal again. Your heart is beating like a drum. Boom. Boom. Boom. Overnight, you got the powers of Monkey D. Luffy from One Piece. Not his straw hat. Not his crew. Just his body. A body made of rubber. Four gears. And one last form, Gear 5, so powerful that it leaves Luffy looking like an old man when it ends. In the next sixty days you'll learn what rubber can really do, what it can't, and why the strongest thing you have also costs the most.

First test: the mirror. You pinch your cheek and pull. It stretches thirty centimetres and snaps back. Real rubber is stretchy, but it has limits. Natural rubber usually breaks when it's stretched to somewhere between four and ten times its length. Luffy goes far beyond that. Your arm just did four metres without tearing. Then you notice something weird. When you stretch, your arm gets warm. When it snaps back, it goes cold. That one's real. It's called the Gough-Joule effect. Stretch a rubber band fast and touch it to your lips, and you'll feel it warm up. Stretching lines up the long molecules inside the rubber, and that releases heat. Letting go does the opposite. So rule one: every stretch makes you warm. A hundred stretches, and you're sweating.

Luffy's most famous move is simple. Stretch your arm back, let go, and it flies forward like a slingshot. You try it on a punching bag. You pull your arm back ten metres. You let go. The bag rips off its chain and lands on the other side of the court. A slingshot stores energy while you stretch it, and gives it all back at once. The further you pull, the more energy. But there's a catch Luffy never mentions. Your arm also has to come back. And it comes back just as fast. You punch yourself in the face with your own elbow. Twice. Rule two: what you throw, comes back.

In One Piece, Luffy beats a man made of lightning, because his rubber body doesn't conduct electricity. So on day six, when a thunderstorm rolls in, you climb to the roof to find out if that's true for you too. Ordinary rubber would lose this fight. A typical lightning bolt carries about three hundred million volts. The thick rubber gloves electricians wear on power lines are rated for thirty-six thousand. Lightning is more than eight thousand times that. Push enough voltage through any insulator and it suddenly gives up and conducts. Physicists call it dielectric breakdown. Even car tyres don't protect you from lightning. What protects you in a car is the metal body, which carries the current around you. But you're not ordinary rubber. The bolt hits the lightning rod next door, jumps to you, and nothing happens. No shock. No pain. Your body is the one thing in this story that follows the manga. Your phone, though, is not made of Nika rubber. And the air around a lightning bolt gets about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. Your hoodie is smoking. Your sneakers have melted to the roof. Rule three: you beat the voltage. You don't beat the heat.

You get a job loading trucks at a frozen-food warehouse, because nobody else can carry that many boxes at once. At minus twenty degrees, your arms feel stiff. Slower. Like old elastic. Real rubber has a breaking point in the cold. Below about minus sixty, natural rubber stops being stretchy and turns hard and brittle, like glass. Only a few places on Earth ever get that cold, deep in Siberia in winter and in Antarctica. Now you know the two places you'll never take a holiday.

A forklift loses its brakes on a ramp and rolls toward a coworker. You do the thing Luffy does when he needs speed. Gear 2. You pump your blood faster, and your skin starts to steam. You move faster than anyone in the warehouse can see. The forklift stops against your hands. Here's what Gear 2 does to a real body. A healthy heart's maximum is roughly two hundred and twenty minus your age. For a twenty-year-old, around two hundred beats a minute. Gear 2 pushes past that, on purpose. In the early story, people warned Luffy that it was shortening his life. You sit on a crate afterwards, your heart racing for an hour. Rule four: speed is borrowed. You pay it back.

Gear 3. You bite your thumb and blow into it, and your bones inflate. Your fist becomes the size of a car. A steel beam slips off a crane. You catch it with a hand as big as a garage door. And then, like early Luffy, your body shrinks back down for a while. You walk home the size of a ten-year-old, in clothes that are far too big. Rule five: big now, small later.

By now there are videos. A forklift. A crane. A kid who suddenly got very small, very fast. Nobody has your name yet. But two people in suits knock on your door and ask, very politely, if you'd like a job. You say no. They leave a card. They don't seem surprised. That night you try the strongest form you know: Gear 4. You blow air into your muscles until they swell like armour, and your arms and legs turn black and hard. In One Piece that black coating is Armament Haki, a willpower that hardens the skin like steel. For about a minute, nothing can touch you. Then it ends, and Luffy's rule hits you too. No armour. No Haki. Ten whole minutes where you're just a tired person in a parking lot.

The next night, you notice a drone. It has been following you since you left the warehouse. The people in suits didn't come back to talk. They came back to watch. And now they know exactly how long your strongest form lasts, and exactly how long you're open afterwards. Ten minutes is enough for a net, a van, and a very quiet drive. So you make a new rule, the one Luffy never needed because he always had his crew: never use Gear 4 alone. Rule six: your strongest minute is also your weakest ten.

Day forty. A gas main explodes under a busy square. Cars tip over. A bus is sliding toward the hole. Your heart starts to drum. Boom. Boom. Boom-boom. Gear 5. Your hair turns white. You laugh, and you don't know why. And the rules stop applying. You turn the road itself into rubber. The bus bounces back onto the street like a toy. You stretch the falling streetlights into soft loops and lay them down gently. For thirty seconds, the whole city is a cartoon, and you are the only one drawing it. The people in the square don't scream. They stare. One woman laughs with you without knowing why. A man films everything with his mouth open. And you can't stop laughing. It isn't happiness exactly. It's the power itself, loud and free, like a song you can't turn off. Somewhere in the back of your head, a quieter voice is counting. Because every second of this is being paid for.

Then it ends. And the cost of Gear 5 arrives all at once, just like it does for Luffy. Your face goes wrinkled. Your legs won't hold you. You look eighty years old, sitting on the curb, laughing at nothing. It wears off by morning. But now you know: the most powerful thing you can do is also the thing you can do least.
