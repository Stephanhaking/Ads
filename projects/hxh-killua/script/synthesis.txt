The storm takes the power out at nine at night, and you're alone in a lift between the seventh floor and the eighth, with the powers of Killua from Hunter x Hunter. The emergency light flickers. The buttons are dead. You put your hand flat on the panel, and something in your arm goes cold, then bright. There's a crack, like a snapped twig. A blue line runs from your fingers into the metal. The lights come back on. The lift starts moving. Nobody saw it. Your hair is standing straight up. In the story, Killua turns his own energy into electricity. Not a gadget, not a glove. Just his body. Over the next forty days you're going to learn what electricity can do inside a person, and what it takes back.

In the story, that energy is called Nen, and it comes in six types. A simple test with a glass of water and a leaf shows which one is yours. You try it at your kitchen table, and the water changes in a way you can't explain. You're a Transmuter, like Killua. You don't make electricity out of nothing. You change what you already have into it. Rule one: the power isn't something you have. It's something your energy turns into.

On day three you try to make a spark on purpose. It works, and it hurts. Your fingers sting for an hour. Killua doesn't feel it the way you do. In the story, he grew up in the Zoldyck family, a family of assassins, and his tolerance for electricity comes from a childhood of training you would never want. You don't have that. You have a stinging hand and a very confused cat. Rule two: the power hits the person who has it first.

On day six you go to the aquarium, because you want to meet something that does this for a living. The electric eel in the tank doesn't look like much. But in 2019, scientists described a new species, Volta's electric eel, that can discharge up to eight hundred and sixty volts. That's more than the roughly six hundred and fifty of the eel everyone already knew about. A fish does that without training, without a family of assassins, without even trying. You stand there for a long time, a little jealous of a fish. Rule three: nature got there first, and it never brags about it.

On day ten, in the park, a kid kicks a football way too hard, straight at a pram. You catch it. And the strange part is, you caught it before you decided to. A normal person needs about a quarter of a second to react to something they see. Studies put it somewhere between about a hundred and ninety and two hundred and eighty-five milliseconds. That's the delay between the eyes, the brain and the muscles. In the story, Killua's first Godspeed move, called Whirlwind, sends electrical signals straight to his muscles, so his body moves without waiting for that delay. Your hand just did the same thing. The mum thanks you. You don't tell her you're as shocked as she is. Rule four: the fastest thing in you isn't your legs. It's the delay you skip.

The second half of Godspeed is called Speed of Lightning: it's about moving at that speed and staying in control. On day fifteen you try it on an empty running track. The start is incredible. The first bend is a disaster. You don't turn, you just keep going, straight into the fence. You lie on the grass looking at the sky, and you realise that being fast was the easy part. Rule five: speed you can't steer is just a crash with extra steps.

On day twenty-two, a friend films your five-a-side game. You're in goal. Someone shoots from two metres away, and you save it. In slow motion, the ball hasn't even left their foot when your hand starts moving. The video goes everywhere. People argue that it's edited, people argue that it isn't, and a sports science lab sends you an email asking if you'd like to come in for a reaction test. You go. You try to be slow on purpose. You're slow on the first three tries, then your body forgets and catches the fourth one before the light even finishes turning green. The researcher doesn't say anything. She just writes for a very long time. Rule six: the world doesn't care how you do it. Only how often.

On day twenty-six your best friend's little brother comes home with a torn jacket and a bruise, and everyone knows who did it. Your friend says it quietly, half joking: you could just give them a little shock. Nobody would ever know. And they're right. Nobody would know. That's what scares you. You think about Killua, a kid raised in a family of assassins. You realise that one shock like that would make you a little bit like them. You say no. Then you do the slow, boring, normal thing: you walk him to school every day that week. Nobody touches him again. Rule seven: a power is only as good as the things you refuse to do with it.

In the story, Godspeed burns through Killua's electricity fast. Using it is easy; running out is the problem. You find that out on day thirty, when you use your power all day just because you can. By evening there's nothing left. No spark, no fast hand. You drop a cup and watch it break, at a normal person's speed. For the first time in a month, you're scared of being ordinary.

On day forty there's a storm again, and it hits the funfair by the river. The power goes out. The ferris wheel stops, with people in every car, and the top ones are swinging in the wind. The engineers say it'll be an hour before the generator works. You put both hands on the control box, and you give it everything you've saved. The wheel moves. One car reaches the platform, and you let it go. Then the next. Then the next. By the last car your arms are shaking, the light in your hands is flickering like a dying bulb, and a little boy steps out holding his dad's hand and waves at the wheel, because he thinks the wheel did it. You let him think that.

The next morning, a door slams in the corridor, and your hand is in the air before you've even heard it. That's the price nobody mentions. Your body reacts before you decide. Every noise, every movement, every time. You can't watch a scary film. You can't be surprised. You have to learn to be slow on purpose, all day, every day. Rule eight: the hardest part of being fast is choosing to wait.
