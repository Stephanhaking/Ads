The scrapyard behind the train station. Nia lands, Kai climbs the fence, and you walk in through the gate like a normal person. On top of a pile of old cars sits a boy with headphones. His name is Ravi. He doesn't get up. He doesn't even look at you. He just lifts one finger. And the car underneath him starts to float. So what if you could move things just by thinking? No hands. No strings.

First, physics wants to talk about the bill. Lifting a car two metres takes about thirty thousand joules of energy. Physics doesn't care if you use your arms, a crane, or your brain. And your brain runs on about twenty watts. Less than an old light bulb. If all of it went into lifting, it would take about twenty-five minutes to raise that car. And you'd have nothing left for thinking. Ravi does it in two seconds. So his power isn't coming from his brain. Nobody knows where it comes from. Not even Ravi.

Here's the second problem. Every push pushes back. That's Newton's third law. When Ravi shoves a car across the yard, something has to push him the other way. That's why he sits down to do it. Standing up, he'd go flying.

Now the fun part. Humans can already move things without touching them. Just not with our minds. In 1997, in the Netherlands, a physicist named Andre Geim put a tiny live frog inside a very powerful magnet. Hundreds of thousands of times stronger than the Earth's magnetic field. The frog floated in mid-air. Unharmed. A magnet that strong pushes on everything a tiny bit. Even water. Even frogs. Even you. Geim won a joke prize for it, the Ig Nobel. Ten years later, he won the real Nobel Prize, for something else. He's the only person who has won both.

And light can push too. Arthur Ashkin built optical tweezers: a laser beam that can grab and hold living bacteria. In 2018, at ninety-six, he won the Nobel Prize for it.

And moving things with your mind? That happened too. In 2012, a woman named Cathy Hutchinson, paralysed by a stroke, had a tiny sensor placed in her brain. She imagined picking up a cup of coffee. A robot arm did it. Her first sip on her own in about fifteen years. Not magic. A sensor, some wires, and a computer. But she moved something with a thought.

That night, Ravi walks with you along the river. He skips stones across the water without throwing them. Nia points at the sky. The star that isn't a star is there again. Brighter. Ravi looks up, and for the first time, he doesn't look bored. Can you push that? Kai asks. Ravi grins. Not yet.
