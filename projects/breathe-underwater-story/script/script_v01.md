## Cold open
[lake at dusk; the hero (red T-shirt) on the pier, friend with blue cap]
You trip on the last plank of the pier and go straight into the lake. Cold. Dark. You open your mouth to scream, and water rushes in.
And nothing happens.
You don't choke. You don't cough. You just breathe. In. Out. Like the lake is air.
Two metres down, you're standing on the bottom. Up on the pier, your friend is losing it. She's been under for a minute! Two minutes! Three!
You wave. He does not wave back.
So what if you could breathe underwater? Not with gills. With your own lungs. Here's how it would actually work.

## The bathtub
[bathroom; mum at the door]
Night one, you test it in the bathtub. Your mum knocks. Why is it so quiet in there? Bubbles. Fine!
And here's what nobody tells you. A litre of air holds about two hundred and nine millilitres of oxygen. A litre of water holds about six. Water carries roughly thirty-six times less.
Your body doesn't care. Sitting still, it burns about two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen every minute. On land, that's about six litres of air.
Underwater it's a different story. Your lungs only grab about a quarter of what goes in, at best. So you'd need about one hundred and seventy-two litres of water. Every minute. While doing nothing.
That's seventeen big buckets of water, through your chest, every single minute.
And water is about eight hundred times denser than air. Each breath isn't a breath. It's a workout. A hundred and seventy-two kilos through your throat. Every minute.
Healthy young men going all out in a breathing test manage about a hundred and forty litres a minute, and only for a short test.
You need more than that just to sit still.
So after ten minutes on the bottom of the lake, your arms are heavy and your head is buzzing. You come up gasping, and for once, the air feels amazing.

## The fish trick
[underwater; a trout glides past]
So you study the experts. A trout drifts past you, totally relaxed. Show-off.
Fish don't breathe in and out. Water goes in the mouth, over the gills, and out the sides. One way. Never stopping.
And inside the gills, the blood runs the opposite way to the water, so it keeps grabbing oxygen the whole way along. Some textbooks say a fish can pull out up to eighty-five percent of it.
Your lungs are a dead end. Same pipe in, same pipe out. Every breath, the old water sitting in your airways mixes with the new.
So you learn to cheat. No more big gulps. You swim slowly with your mouth open, like the trout, always moving. It's not perfect. But now you can stay down for an hour.
Your friend calls it your fish face.

## The cold
[overcast lake; autumn]
Then autumn comes.
The first time you jump in after summer, your body gasps on its own. That's cold shock. And water pulls heat out of you fast. About twenty-five times faster than air, one safety guide says. Other sources say anywhere from four to thirty-two. Either way, faster.
Your hands go numb in minutes. And every breath is cold lake water, straight into your chest.
So you buy a wetsuit. Your friend says you look like a seal. He's not wrong.

## How deep?
[deep water; a depth rope]
Then you get curious. How deep can you go?
Every ten metres, the water squeezes you with one more atmosphere. At thirty metres, that's four times what you feel at the surface.
Divers breathing air down here face a danger called narcosis, one of the biggest risks in diving. And if they come up too fast, bubbles form in their blood. The bends.
But you're not breathing air. You're breathing water. Would that happen to you?
Honestly? Nobody knows. Nobody has ever tested it.
You decide not to be the first.

## The ice
[winter; frozen lake edge; kids on the ice]
Then one morning in January, you hear it before you see it. A crack, like a branch snapping. Your little cousin, out on the ice where the signs say don't.
And then he's gone.
You don't think. You run, you jump, you're under the ice.
It's black down here. Freezing. He's a few metres away, kicking, gasping. That's the cold shock.
You grab his jacket. You can't see the hole. Up is ice, ice, ice.
Then you see it. A grey circle of light. You swim, and you push, and he's out.
Six minutes. That's how long they say you were under.
The paramedics wrap him in a silver blanket. He's shaking, but he's talking. He's going to be fine.
Your friend stares at you. How were you down there for six minutes? I held my breath. Liar.

## The message
[night; bedroom; phone]
That night, your phone buzzes. Unknown number.
We saw what you did under the ice. You're not the only one.

## Could it be real?
[lab, then hospital exterior]
But could a human really breathe liquid?
Scientists have tried. In the nineteen-sixties, mice in a lab breathed liquid packed with oxygen. In nineteen eighty-nine, doctors in Philadelphia put a special liquid into the lungs of three tiny premature babies, with a ventilator still helping. Their lungs got easier to inflate.
In nineteen ninety-six, a study of thirteen babies saw their blood oxygen jump by a hundred and thirty-eight percent within an hour.
Not breathing underwater. But the first time a liquid helped humans breathe.
