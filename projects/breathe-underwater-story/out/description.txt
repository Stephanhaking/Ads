What If You Could Breathe Underwater?

You fall off a pier and find out you can breathe lake water. Not with gills, with your own lungs. This story follows what would really happen next: the bathtub test, the arithmetic of oxygen in water, a trick borrowed from fish, the cold, the depth, a rescue under the ice, and the real experiments where liquid has helped people breathe.

The story and the characters are fiction. The numbers are real (sources below). Please don't try to breathe water, and never walk on thin ice.

THE NUMBERS AND WHERE THEY COME FROM
- Oxygen in air: 20.9 %, about 209 mL per litre (FAO, fao.org/3/ac183e/ac183e04.htm)
- Oxygen in air-saturated water at 25 °C: 8.3 mg/L, about 5.8 mL per litre (dissolved-oxygen sensor white paper, wateronline.com). Colder water holds a little more.
- Resting oxygen use: about 250 mL a minute for an adult (respiratory physiology references)
- Lungs take up roughly a seventh to a quarter of the oxygen breathed in (exhaled oxygen 16–18 % vs 21 % inhaled; arXiv 2005.12362). We used a quarter, the generous end: 250 ÷ 5.8 ÷ 0.25 ≈ 172 L of water a minute (our arithmetic).
- Maximal voluntary ventilation: about 140 L/min for young men in a short test (Monash University physiology module)
- Water is about 800 times denser than air
- Fish gills: sources give 70 % to over 90 % oxygen extraction; "up to 85 %" is a textbook figure
- Cold water: heat loss often quoted as about 25 times faster than air; other sources say 4 to 32 times. Cold shock gasp; hands numb within minutes (water-safety guides)
- Pressure: about one more atmosphere per 10 m of water; narcosis and decompression sickness are diving hazards for divers breathing air. Whether a liquid breather would get the bends is untested.
- 1960s: mice breathed oxygenated liquids in lab experiments (Kylstra; Clark and Gollan). Accounts of how long they survived differ.
- 1989: partial liquid ventilation in three premature infants in Philadelphia improved lung compliance (Greenspan et al., J Pediatr 1990;117:106-111)
- 1996: a study of 13 infants saw blood oxygen (PaO2) rise by 138 % on average within an hour (NEJM, 1996). Later trials did not show lower illness rates in acute respiratory failure.

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; the original papers were not all read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
