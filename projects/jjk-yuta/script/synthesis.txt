You've woken up with Yuta Okkotsu's Copy, from Jujutsu Kaisen. In real life. It can copy anything you watch someone do. And the thing standing behind you decides what it costs. In forty days, five of the things you've copied will save fourteen people in a flooded tunnel. But right now it's eleven forty-seven at night, there's a ring on your finger you've never seen, and something huge is breathing behind you.

In the story, the ring was a promise. Rika Orimoto was Yuta's friend when they were children. She gave him her mother's ring and promised to marry him. Then she died young. Yuta couldn't let go. Her soul stayed, tied to his cursed energy, and it became a special-grade curse, the Queen of Curses. Now it's behind you. Pale, huge, all teeth, and standing very close. Your mum walks past the door and says goodnight to the wardrobe. It doesn't attack. It just looks at the ring. Lesson one: it isn't a weapon. It's a promise with a very big shadow.

In the story, Copy lets Yuta reproduce other people's techniques. And nobody is completely sure of the exact conditions. So, at breakfast, you watch your dad flip an omelette. You've never flipped one in your life. Your hands do it. Perfectly. The little tilt of the wrist, the fold at the end, even the way he blows a strand of hair out of his eyes. You try the second one on your own, and it's a disaster. Lesson two: you copy what you see, not what you understand.

At lunch you stop in front of a busker with a guitar. You watch his hands for one song. Then you borrow the guitar and play his whole piece, note for note, the little slides and all. A small crowd stops. Somebody films it. He stares at you like you've stolen something, and he isn't entirely wrong. Then your fingers start to shake. They can do it, but they've never done it before, and the body notices. Lesson three: copying a skill isn't the same as being built for it.

In the evening you watch a climber on a wall, then copy every move. Halfway up, you realise you copied his reach but not his arms. You slip. Something huge and pale grabs you by the hood, in mid-air, and sets you down very gently. It looks at you. It doesn't look pleased. You never find out if that was a rescue or a warning. Lesson four: she's not your servant. Sometimes she catches you anyway.

On day three, you have to give a presentation at work. In the film, Yuta uses Cursed Speech, the technique that belongs to Toge Inumaki's family. It's normally passed down only inside that clan. You don't have that. But you've copied your old teacher's voice, the one that made a whole classroom go quiet. You open your mouth, and the room stops. It's a little too good. A colleague goes pale and asks you not to do that again. Lesson five: when you copy a voice, you copy what it does to other people.

On day six, your friend says something odd. You've started talking like the man who shouted at you on the bus. You didn't decide it. You just watched him, and it stuck. In nineteen sixty-one, the psychologist Albert Bandura showed that children who watched an adult hit an inflatable doll later did the same, without being told to. We copy more than we think. You count how many strangers you've copied this week. You stop at twenty. Lesson six: you don't choose everything you copy.

A boy films you in the park: first juggling, then a handstand, then a perfect cartwheel. He posts it. People say it's editing. People say you're hiding something. Nobody says "Rika", because nobody knows who that is, and the few who do think it's a joke. Lesson seven: the ones who'd understand are the ones who'd never believe it.

On day twelve you watch a dancer rehearse in a studio, through the window, for twenty minutes. Then you walk in and do the whole routine, every turn, every pause, as if you'd practised it for years. Her teacher claps. She wants you in the show on Friday. On Friday, the lights come on, and you freeze. Your feet know every step. Your stomach doesn't. Someone huge and pale stands in the wings, and you could swear she's covering her face. Lesson eight: you can copy the steps, but not the nerves that go with them.

On day fifteen, an old chess player lets you sit down across from him. You copy his opening, move for move. It's beautiful. Then he makes a move you've never seen, and you don't know why. You lose in eleven turns. He smiles. "You have my hands," he says. "You don't have my reasons." Lesson nine: Copy shows you the move. It never shows you the why.

On day seventeen you notice something. Every skill you copy stays in your hands, but not all at once. You can hold five of them at a time. The sixth pushes the oldest one out, like a drawer that's too full. You write the five on a sheet of paper, and stick it on the wardrobe, because you keep forgetting. It looks like a very strange shopping list. Your mum asks what "locksmith, swimmer, climber, driver, strongman" is for. You say: "Just in case." Lesson ten: choose carefully what you keep. You can't keep everything.

On day twenty you take the ring off, to see what happens. In the story, Yuta calls Rika out using that ring. And her cursed energy is vast. The lights go out. The room gets very cold. Something huge unfolds behind you, taller than the ceiling, and the walls start to hum. You put the ring back on. It all folds away, slowly. She never moved from where she was. Lesson eleven: the ring isn't a leash. It's a door, and you're the only one who can close it.

On day twenty-five, a man collapses on a pavement in front of you. People step back. Someone is already shouting for an ambulance. Months ago, on a training video, you watched a paramedic do chest compressions. You've never done one. You kneel down. Your hands do it. Steady, strong, in rhythm, and you keep going while your arms burn. You don't know why it works. You only know it works until the real paramedics arrive. They take over, look at you, and ask where you trained. Lesson twelve: sometimes you don't need to understand the move. You only need to buy someone time.

By day thirty you have a hundred skills in your hands, and not one you can explain. You're tired in a way sleep doesn't fix. You can do anything you've watched, and you've forgotten how to do the things you used to love. You find your old guitar. You can't play it any more. Only the busker's song. Lesson thirteen: you can borrow a lot, but you can't borrow a self.

On day forty, the rain is so heavy that an underpass floods around a stopped bus, with fourteen people inside. You're the only one who can do anything. You have a hundred skills in your hands, but only five fit. You swap them in your head, the way you'd swap cards: the old chess player goes, the dancer goes, the busker goes. The swimmer's stroke, to get across. The climber's grip, to reach the roof. The driver's calm, to open the door. The locksmith's fingers, for the jammed window. The strongman's back, to hold it. You put the ring on your finger and say nothing. There isn't time to say anything. Something huge and pale rises behind you, and the water goes very still. For the first time, it doesn't look at the ring. It looks at the bus. You get them out one by one. Fourteen people on a wet pavement, shaking, wrapped in whatever coats were nearby. Nobody asks what you are.

The next morning you can't lift your arms, and she's sitting at the foot of your bed. In the story, after the film, Rika's soul moves on, and Yuta keeps using her power. Yours doesn't move on. She stays. You say thank you, out loud, to a wardrobe, and for a moment the ring feels warm. Lesson fourteen: the strongest thing you copied wasn't a skill. It was a promise to keep going.

The next night, at eleven forty-seven, you look at the ring on your finger. So if you woke up tomorrow with Yuta's Copy, what's the first thing you'd copy? And would you take off the ring, to see what's behind it? Tell me in the comments.
