## Breathe underwater (short, 9:16)

[3D clay: a swimmer sinks beside a pier, camera follows down; tag "2 m"; badge SIMULATION]
Two metres under a lake. A person, breathing.
Say a person could breathe water the way a fish does. Not a fact. A premise.

[Stat cards: 209 mL vs 6 mL, drawn as two jars; footnote "FAO; sensor-maker white paper, 25 °C"]
A litre of air holds about two hundred and nine millilitres of oxygen. A litre of water holds about six. Roughly thirty-six times less.

[Counter: 250 mL per minute; lungs animation; footnote "resting adult, physiology course data"]
At rest, a body uses about two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen a minute.
From air, that is about six litres of breathing.
From water, even if the lungs took every molecule, forty-three litres.

[Clay mannequin at a surface of water, bucket stack counts 172 L; tag "ASSUMPTION: lungs take a quarter"]
Lungs don't take every molecule. Say they took a quarter. That is our assumption, not a measurement.
One hundred and seventy-two litres a minute.

[Chart: bar of 172 vs the measured maximum ~140; footnote "Monash physiology module"]
A Monash physiology module puts the maximum a young man can breathe, in a short test, at about a hundred and forty litres a minute.
The requirement is higher than the maximum. At rest.

[Scale: 172 kg of water, a clay crate, tag "800x denser than air"]
And water is about eight hundred times denser than air. A hundred and seventy-two litres is a hundred and seventy-two kilograms. Through the airways. Every minute.

[Map card + statement: "1960s · animal experiments · not human"]
So what about liquid? In the 1960s, according to a Gizmodo history, Johannes Kylstra's team at Duke kept mice breathing oxygenated saline under heavy pressure. They died minutes later, from carbon dioxide.
Clark and Gollan's mice breathed a fluorocarbon liquid at normal pressure. Rats, the same history says, stayed under for up to twenty hours.
Whether a healthy adult could do that for hours has not been shown.

[3D: back to the lake, slow pull-out]
Two metres under a lake. Six litres of air, or a hundred and seventy-two of water.
The body asks for the first.
