Your phone slips out of your hand on a balcony twelve floors up, and it stops. Not in your hand. In the air, a metre below the railing, screen still glowing. A pigeon hangs over the street with its wings half open. The smoke from a chimney is a grey sculpture. The cars below are parked in the middle of the road. And there is no sound at all. You reach down, take your phone out of the air, and count. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. And the city starts again, like someone pressed play.

Overnight, you got the power of The World, the time-stopping ability from JoJo's Bizarre Adventure. Not Dio's mansion. Not his knives. Just this: you can stop time for everyone except you. In the story, it starts at about five seconds. Over the next forty days, you're going to learn what five seconds is really worth.

Breakfast. Your elbow knocks a glass of orange juice off the table. You don't even think. Everything stops. The juice hangs in the air like an orange ribbon, half in the glass, half out. You walk around it. You poke it, and it's oddly stiff, like jelly. You put the glass back under it, and when time starts, the juice falls right back in. You try again. And again. You count every time, and it's always the same: five seconds, then the world moves. Rule one: you get five seconds. Not six. Not "a bit longer if you really need it". Five.

On day two you stop time in the middle of a busy street, and the first thing that hits you isn't the frozen people. It's the silence. Sound is just air bumping into air. In normal life it moves at about 343 metres per second, passed from molecule to molecule. The molecules themselves are flying around at about 500 metres per second. In your five seconds, none of them move. So nothing reaches your ears. Not the guitar, not the dog, not your own footsteps. You clap your hands. Nothing. You shout. Nothing. It's the quietest place you have ever been, and it's in the middle of a city. Rule two: frozen time is silent. If you want to talk to someone, you have to wait.

On day four you test the strangest part. You stop time in a park, move a frisbee two metres to the left, and let go. The dog jumps for where the frisbee was. Then it looks around, confused. But nobody else even blinks. That's because a human needs about a quarter of a second just to react to something they see. Five seconds is about twenty of those. You can do a lot in twenty reactions, and to everyone else it looks like one. Rule three: from the outside, there's no gap. Things just jump.

On day seven you go to a running track to find out how far five seconds can take you. The fastest man ever, Usain Bolt, ran 100 metres in 9.58 seconds. That's an average of about ten metres per second. In five seconds, even he would only get about fifty metres. You are not Usain Bolt. You stop time, run as hard as you can, and when it restarts you're a lot less than fifty metres away, out of breath, with your shoelace untied. Rule four: stopping time doesn't make you faster. It only makes everyone else slower.

On day eleven you film yourself. Phone on a tripod, you stop time, walk across the room, and let go. On the video, you are on the left, and then, in a single frame, you are on the right. No blur. No in-between. You just teleport. And here's a real thing that sounds made up: in 2013, physicists in Darmstadt, in Germany, managed to hold light still inside a crystal for up to a minute, and store an image in it. So the light around you in your frozen world isn't impossible. It's just very, very hard. Rule five: cameras never see the frozen part. They only see the jump.

Someone else filmed it too. On day eighteen, a video called "Glitch Man" has nine million views. A person on a pedestrian crossing is on one side of a bus, and in the next frame they're on the other side. Half the comments say it's editing. The other half say it's a time traveller. Nobody says "time stopper", because that's not a real thing. And you notice what everyone notices first: how easy it would be to take things. A wallet. An exam answer. A card at a casino table. You walk past the casino. Rule six: if it isn't yours in normal time, it isn't yours in frozen time.

On day twenty-five, two people in grey suits find you. Very polite. They have a room full of stopwatches and a laser beam you're supposed to cross without breaking. You stop time, step through the beam, and stand on the other side. To them, you just appear. They do it again and again, all afternoon. And something happens that you didn't expect: the more you practise, the longer it gets. Five seconds. Six. Seven. In the story, Dio pushes his time stop to about nine seconds, according to the Wikipedia summary. On day thirty-nine, in a white room, with a woman holding a clipboard, you reach nine. She writes one line in her report: "Duration: increasing."

On day forty, you're walking home when an apartment block catches fire. A family is stuck on a fourth-floor balcony. The fire escape is gone. You stop time. The flames look like orange glass. The smoke is grey cotton you can push aside with your hand. It's the most beautiful thing you have ever seen, and you only have nine seconds. You get up the stairs inside the building in four. You pick up the youngest child. Two seconds to the window. Three to the ground. Time starts. The crowd sees a child appear on the pavement and screams. You wait. You catch your breath. You stop it again. Nine seconds at a time, you bring down the whole family, one by one. To everyone outside, it takes less than a minute. For you, it takes much longer, and every one of those seconds is silent, and yours alone.

This is the part nobody warns you about. Every time you stop the world, you keep living. Your heart beats, your hair grows, you get a little older. The rest of the world doesn't. Over forty days you've lived hours that nobody else lived. And you spent all of them alone, in a silent city where nobody could answer you. The next day you meet a friend for coffee. Halfway through a story, they start to laugh, the big kind of laugh where they can't finish the sentence. And you realise you could freeze this. You could walk around it and look at it for nine seconds. You don't. Rule seven: the moments worth keeping are the ones you share. You can't share a moment you've stopped.
