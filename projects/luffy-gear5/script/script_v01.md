# Pilot: What If You Had Luffy's Powers in Real Life? (Gear 5)
# Draft v01 — about 9 minutes (~1,500 words at ~170 wpm). Our structure: power in the first line, a day counter,
# one new scene about every minute, each power with a rule, a cost, and a real, sourced fact.

## Cold open (0:00)
[subway stairs, rush hour; the hero slips; their arm shoots four metres across the station and grabs a railing]
You miss the last step of the subway stairs. You're falling backwards into a crowd of strangers.
And then your arm stretches four metres across the station, wraps around a railing, and pulls you back like a bungee cord.
Nobody saw it. Everybody was looking at their phone.
You look at your hand. It's normal again. Your heart is beating like a drum. Boom. Boom. Boom.
Overnight, you got the powers of Monkey D. Luffy from One Piece. Not his straw hat. Not his crew. Just his body.
A body made of rubber. Four gears. And one last form, Gear 5, so powerful that it leaves Luffy looking like an old man when it ends.
In the next sixty days you'll learn what rubber can really do, what it can't, and why the strongest thing you have also costs the most.

## Day 1: the rules of rubber (0:50)
[bathroom mirror; the hero pulls their cheek and it stretches]
First test: the mirror. You pinch your cheek and pull. It stretches thirty centimetres and snaps back.
Real rubber is stretchy, but it has limits. Natural rubber usually breaks when it's stretched to somewhere between four and ten times its length.
Luffy goes far beyond that. Your arm just did four metres without tearing.
Then you notice something weird. When you stretch, your arm gets warm. When it snaps back, it goes cold.
That one's real. It's called the Gough-Joule effect. Stretch a rubber band fast and touch it to your lips, and you'll feel it warm up.
Stretching lines up the long molecules inside the rubber, and that releases heat. Letting go does the opposite.
So rule one: every stretch makes you warm. A hundred stretches, and you're sweating.

## Day 3: the punch (1:55)
[an empty basketball court at dawn; a punching bag]
Luffy's most famous move is simple. Stretch your arm back, let go, and it flies forward like a slingshot.
You try it on a punching bag. You pull your arm back ten metres. You let go.
The bag rips off its chain and lands on the other side of the court.
A slingshot stores energy while you stretch it, and gives it all back at once. The further you pull, the more energy.
But there's a catch Luffy never mentions. Your arm also has to come back. And it comes back just as fast.
You punch yourself in the face with your own elbow. Twice.
Rule two: what you throw, comes back.

## Day 6: the storm (3:00)
[a thunderstorm over the city; the hero on a rooftop, thinking]
In One Piece, Luffy beats a man made of lightning, because rubber doesn't conduct electricity.
So on day six, when a thunderstorm rolls in, you have a very bad idea. You go up to the roof.
Then you remember the numbers, and you go back down.
A typical lightning bolt carries about three hundred million volts.
The thick rubber gloves electricians use on power lines are rated for thirty-six thousand volts.
Lightning is more than eight thousand times that.
And here's the myth: car tyres don't protect you from lightning either. What protects you in a car is the metal body, which carries the current around you and into the ground.
Rubber that beats lightning only works in the manga. Rule three: you are not lightning-proof. Stay inside.

## Day 9: the cold (4:00)
[a frozen-food warehouse; breath clouds; the hero in a work jacket]
You get a job loading trucks at a frozen-food warehouse, because nobody else can carry that many boxes at once.
At minus twenty degrees, your arms feel stiff. Slower. Like old elastic.
Real rubber has a breaking point in the cold. Below about minus sixty, natural rubber stops being stretchy and turns hard and brittle, like glass.
Only a few places on Earth ever get that cold, deep in Siberia in winter and in Antarctica. Now you know the two places you'll never take a holiday.

## Day 14: Gear 2 (4:55)
[the warehouse; a runaway forklift; steam rising from the hero's skin]
A forklift loses its brakes on a ramp and rolls toward a coworker.
You do the thing Luffy does when he needs speed. Gear 2. You pump your blood faster, and your skin starts to steam.
You move faster than anyone in the warehouse can see. The forklift stops against your hands.
Here's what Gear 2 does to a real body. A healthy heart's maximum is roughly two hundred and twenty minus your age. For a twenty-year-old, around two hundred beats a minute.
Gear 2 pushes past that, on purpose. In the early story, people warned Luffy that it was shortening his life.
You sit on a crate afterwards, your heart racing for an hour. Rule four: speed is borrowed. You pay it back.

## Day 20: Gear 3 (5:55)
[a construction site; a falling steel beam; the hero's fist inflating like a giant balloon]
Gear 3. You bite your thumb and blow into it, and your bones inflate. Your fist becomes the size of a car.
A steel beam slips off a crane. You catch it with a hand as big as a garage door.
And then, like early Luffy, your body shrinks back down for a while. You walk home the size of a ten-year-old, in clothes that are far too big.
Rule five: big now, small later.

## Day 27: Gear 4 (6:45)
[a video spreading across phones; a news ticker; two people in suits at the door]
By now there are videos. A forklift. A crane. A boy who got very small, very fast.
Nobody has your name yet. But two people in suits knock on your door and ask, very politely, if you'd like a job.
You say no. That night you try the strongest form you know: Gear 4. Muscles inflated, arms coated in black armour.
It's incredible for about a minute. Then it ends, and Luffy's rule hits you too. No armour, nothing, for ten whole minutes.
Ten minutes is a long time when people in suits know your address.

## Day 40: Gear 5 (7:45)
[night; a city square; a drumbeat; the hero's hair turns white]
Day forty. A gas main explodes under a busy square. Cars tip over. A bus is sliding toward the hole.
Your heart starts to drum. Boom. Boom. Boom-boom.
Gear 5. Your hair turns white. You laugh, and you don't know why.
And the rules stop applying. You turn the road itself into rubber. The bus bounces back onto the street like a toy.
You stretch the falling streetlights into soft loops and lay them down gently.
For thirty seconds, the whole city is a cartoon, and you are the only one drawing it.

## The cost (8:35)
[the hero sitting on the curb; wrinkled face; white hair; very tired]
Then it ends. And the cost of Gear 5 arrives all at once, just like it does for Luffy.
Your face goes wrinkled. Your legs won't hold you. You look eighty years old, sitting on the curb, laughing at nothing.
It wears off by morning. But now you know: the most powerful thing you can do is also the thing you can do least.
