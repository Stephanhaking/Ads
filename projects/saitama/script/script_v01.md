# What If You Had Saitama's Powers in Real Life?
# Draft v01 — Curious-Dude-style structure (second person, a physical hook in the first seconds, a day counter, a new scene about
# every minute, every power with a rule and a cost, the world reacting, an emotional close that loops back to the opening).
# Our own words and scenes. Science kept to a few one-line touches (facts from research/facts.md: R01, R02, C01, R04, R05, R07).
# ~1,450 spoken words, about 9 minutes at ~160 wpm.

## Cold open (0:00)
[a dark bedroom; a phone alarm; a hand reaches out]
6:15 in the morning. Your alarm goes off, and you tap the snooze button the way you always do.
The phone goes through the nightstand. Then through the floor. You hear it land in your downstairs neighbour's kitchen, still ringing.

You sit up. Something feels cold. You touch your head. There's nothing there. Not a single hair.

Sometime during the night, you got the power of Saitama. One-Punch Man. No transformation, no special moves, no gears, no glowing aura. Just this: anything you hit with real intent goes down in one punch. Anything.

Within a few months you'll be the strongest person who has ever lived. And you'll find out that the hardest thing about being unbeatable isn't the fighting. It's everything after.

## Day 1: the rules of one punch (0:45)
[the bathroom; a toothbrush; the sink; the kitchen; a mug]
First problem: everything in your apartment is made of paper now.
You squeeze the toothpaste and it empties in one shot. You turn the tap and it comes off in your hand. You pick up a mug and you're holding a handle.

So you learn rule one, the way Saitama lives it: your strength doesn't turn off. You do. You move slowly. You hold things like they're eggs. Door handles get one finger. By lunchtime you can make a cup of tea without destroying the kettle, and you have never been prouder of anything.

And the hair. Real stress hair loss takes two to four months to show up, and it usually grows back. Yours took one night. And it's not coming back.

## Day 2: the gym (1:45)
[a small gym; a heavy bag; a nervous trainer]
You need to know how strong you are, so you go to the quietest gym in town and ask to use the heavy bag. The trainer laughs. You tap it. Barely.
The bag doesn't swing. It tears off the chain, goes through the wall, and lands in the car park outside.

So you learn rule two. With Saitama there are two kinds of punches: the normal one, which is already too much, and the serious one, which you never want to find out about indoors.

You go home and practise holding back on a pillow. Then on a mattress. Then you buy a new mattress.

## Day 5: faster than sound (2:40)
[an empty field at dawn; the hero alone; a single punch at the air]
On day five you test your speed somewhere safe: an empty field, nobody around. You punch the air. Not at anything. Just the air.
There's a crack like a gunshot, and every car alarm in the street behind you goes off.

The fastest punches ever measured in a lab hit about 12 metres per second. To make that crack, your fist broke the sound barrier, around 343 metres per second. That's nearly thirty times faster, and about eight hundred times the energy. You punched the sky, and the sky complained.

Somebody filmed it from a balcony.

## Day 9: everybody has a video (3:35)
[phones; a split screen of comments; a news ticker; the hero in a hoodie and cap]
By day nine the video has twelve million views. The comments are split right down the middle. Half of them say it's CGI. The other half say it's CGI, but good CGI.
Nobody believes you. That's the strange part. You can stop a truck, but you can't stop people saying you're fake.

You find out that being strong in real life doesn't come with an audience. It comes with a landlord asking who is paying for the wall.

## Day 17: the first real emergency (4:25)
[a city street; a crane; a falling load of steel beams; people running]
On day seventeen you're walking home from the supermarket when a crane on a building site snaps. A load of steel beams is falling toward a bus stop full of people.

You don't think. You jump. You catch the first beam, punch the second one sideways into the empty lot, and set the third one down on the pavement like a sofa.
Nobody is hurt. It takes about four seconds.

When the news arrives, a man in a shiny costume who arrived ten minutes later is giving interviews. You're holding a bag of groceries with the eggs still intact. You decide that counts as a win.

## Day 30: they want to measure you (5:15)
[a testing hall; men in suits; a pressure plate; a steel block; a broken machine]
On day thirty, two people in grey suits knock on your door. Very polite. They'd like to run a few tests.
They bring a machine that measures punching force. You hit it gently. It stops working. They bring a bigger one. Same thing. They bring a block of steel the size of a fridge, and it folds like a napkin.

At the end of the day they don't have a number. They have a report with one line: "Upper limit: not found."

And you notice something. You're not nervous. You're not even curious. You're checking the time, because the supermarket has a sale at six.

## Day 45: the challengers (6:00)
[a queue of fighters; a rooftop; one tap each]
Word gets around. Fighters start showing up. Boxers, wrestlers, a man who has trained in the mountains for twelve years.
Each of them gets one tap. You start apologising before you even swing. One of them asks you to fight seriously, just once. You can't. If you did, there would be no one left to ask.

## Day 80: the cost (6:40)
[the hero alone at home; a video game; an empty sofa; rain on the window]
This is the part nobody warns you about.
At first, winning felt amazing. Then it felt normal. Then it felt like nothing. Psychologists call it hedonic adaptation: we get used to almost anything, good or bad, and the thrill fades. Saitama's real enemy was never a monster. It was boredom.

Every fight ends before it starts. Every video game feels slow. Every danger looks like it's standing still. You'd give anything for one opponent who makes you sweat, just once, to remember what it felt like to try.

## Day 120: the meteor (7:25)
[a red sky; a burning rock; the city panicking; the hero on a rooftop]
And then, on day one hundred and twenty, the sky turns red. Something is coming in from space, a rock the size of a stadium, and every government on Earth runs out of time at the same moment.
For the first time in months, your heart is pounding.

You jump. You meet it above the clouds, and for one second you throw a serious punch.
The rock turns into gravel and dust. A few pieces still rain down on the city. A few roofs get holes, a few cars get dents. Nobody dies.

The next morning a headline asks who will pay for the damage.

## Day 121: back to normal (8:20)
[the supermarket; a sale sign; a carton of eggs; then the bedroom at night]
So you do what Saitama does. You go shopping. Eggs are half price, and for some reason that makes you happier than saving the planet did.

That night, you set your alarm for 6:15. In the morning it rings, and you reach out and tap it. Gently.
It stops ringing. The nightstand is still there.
It's the hardest thing you've learned all year.

## Ending question (8:55)
[the hero, small against a big sky; the counter "DAY 121"; "YOUR TURN"]
So: would you take one punch that wins every fight, if it meant nothing would ever feel hard again?
And what's the first thing you'd punch? Tell us in the comments.
