## Cold open
[the dark alley from last time; the stranger in the dark hoodie, their dog; the hero with sunglasses, visible again]
The alley. The stranger. The dog. And you, standing there in your sunglasses, trying very hard not to panic.
Don't think about the toothbrush, you tell yourself. Don't think about the toothbrush.
The stranger tilts their head. Toothbrush?
Your stomach drops. They can hear you. Every thought.
Relax, they say. I'm not here to hurt you. I just wanted to see if you'd follow me. You did.
So what if you could read minds? Not guess. Actually hear what people think.

## The signal
[a brain made of glowing dots; a skull; tiny sparks]
Here's what you'd be listening to. Your brain has about eighty-six billion nerve cells, called neurons.
Every thought you have is millions of them firing tiny electrical pulses.
Sounds loud. It isn't. By the time those signals reach the outside of your skull, they're about fifty millionths of a volt.
The magnetic field your brain makes is weaker still. One researcher puts it at more than a billion times weaker than the Earth's.
To measure it, scientists use super-cold sensors inside rooms shielded from the outside world. A passing car can drown it out.
So hearing thoughts from across a street? With known physics, that's impossible.

## The noise
[the night market from the alley mouth; speech bubbles of thoughts everywhere]
But the stranger can. And they tell you what it's like.
Imagine a crowded market, they say. Now imagine hearing every single person, all at once.
Did I leave the oven on. I hate this song. Is that dog looking at me.
It never stops. That's why I like alleys.

## Mind reading for real
[a hospital research room; an MRI scanner; a screen of words]
And here's the strange part. Scientists are starting to do a tiny version of it.
In 2023, a team at the University of Texas put people inside a brain scanner while they listened to podcasts. For about sixteen hours each.
A computer learned which brain patterns went with which ideas. Then, when people imagined telling a story, it turned their brain activity into text.
Not word for word. It got the gist.
But it only worked on the person it was trained on. And people could block it, just by counting, or by thinking about animals.

## The test
[the alley; the hero thinking hard, little animal doodles floating above her head]
So you try it. You think as hard as you can. Penguin. Giraffe. Hamster. Penguin.
The stranger winces. Okay, okay. Stop. That's so annoying.
It works on them too.

## Talking without moving
[a bright rehabilitation room; a woman in a wheelchair; a talking avatar on a screen]
Brain implants go even further.
In 2023, doctors in San Francisco placed a thin sheet of sensors on the brain of a woman named Ann. A stroke had left her unable to speak for eighteen years.
The sensors picked up the signals for the sounds she was trying to make. A computer turned them into words, spoken by an avatar on a screen.
About seventy-eight words a minute. Normal conversation is about a hundred and sixty. But for Ann, it was a voice again.
And in 2024, the first person with a Neuralink implant moved a cursor and played chess on a laptop, just by thinking.

## Who they are
[the alley; the stranger lowers their hood; behind them, four silhouettes appear on the rooftops]
The stranger lowers their hood. My name is Kai, they say. And I'm not the only one.
Behind them, on the rooftops, four shapes are watching.
We sent you the message after the lake, Kai says. We saw what you did under the ice. In the gym. At the crossing.
There are more of us. And something's coming that we can't handle on our own.
Tomorrow. Six a.m. The old observatory on the hill.
Then Kai smiles. And stop thinking about penguins. It's distracting.
