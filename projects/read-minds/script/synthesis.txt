Here's what you'd be listening to. Your brain has about eighty-six billion nerve cells, called neurons. Every thought you have is millions of them firing tiny electrical pulses. Sounds loud. It isn't. By the time those signals reach the outside of your skull, they're about fifty millionths of a volt. The magnetic field your brain makes is weaker still. One researcher puts it at more than a billion times weaker than the Earth's. To measure it, scientists use super-cold sensors inside rooms shielded from the outside world. A passing car can drown it out. So hearing thoughts from across a street? With known physics, that's impossible.

But the stranger can. And they tell you what it's like. Imagine a crowded market, they say. Now imagine hearing every single person, all at once. Did I leave the oven on. I hate this song. Is that dog looking at me. It never stops. That's why I like alleys.

And here's the strange part. Scientists are starting to do a tiny version of it. In 2023, a team at the University of Texas put people inside a brain scanner while they listened to podcasts. For about sixteen hours each. A computer learned which brain patterns went with which ideas. Then, when people imagined telling a story, it turned their brain activity into text. Not word for word. It got the gist. But it only worked on the person it was trained on. And people could block it, just by counting, or by thinking about animals.

Brain implants go even further. In 2023, doctors in San Francisco placed a thin sheet of sensors on the brain of a woman named Ann. A stroke had left her unable to speak for eighteen years. The sensors picked up the signals for the sounds she was trying to make. A computer turned them into words, spoken by an avatar on a screen. About seventy-eight words a minute. Normal conversation is about a hundred and sixty. But for Ann, it was a voice again. And in 2024, the first person with a Neuralink implant moved a cursor and played chess on a laptop, just by thinking.
