What If You Could Read Minds?

The stranger from the frozen street can hear every thought you have. This story follows what mind-reading would really involve: how faint the brain's signals are outside your head, why hearing a crowd would be unbearable, and the real scanners and implants that already turn brain activity into words.

The story (and Kai's power) is fiction. The science is real (sources below).

THE SCIENCE AND WHERE IT COMES FROM
- About 86 billion neurons in a human brain, with roughly 100 trillion synapses (estimates vary) (UCLA Brain Research Institute; Herculano-Houzel 2009)
- Scalp EEG signals are around 50 microvolts and must be amplified thousands of times (HyperPhysics)
- The brain's magnetic field is in the femtotesla range — one researcher puts it at more than a billion times weaker than Earth's; MEG uses super-cooled sensors in magnetically shielded rooms (University of Oldenburg; Karolinska Institutet)
- 2023, University of Texas at Austin (Tang, LeBel, Jain, Huth; Nature Neuroscience): an fMRI "semantic decoder" trained on about 16 hours of podcasts per person turned brain activity into text that captured the gist, not exact words; it failed on people it wasn't trained on, and participants could sabotage it by counting or naming animals (NIH Research Matters; University of Sydney)
- 2023, UCSF (Edward Chang's team; Nature): a woman named Ann, paralysed by a brain-stem stroke 18 years earlier, communicated through a talking avatar at about 78 words per minute; natural conversation is about 160 (Scientific American)
- 2024: the first Neuralink patient moved a cursor and played online chess using his implant (AP / Reuters reports)

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; not every original paper was read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
