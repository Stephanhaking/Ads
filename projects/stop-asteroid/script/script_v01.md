## Cold open
[a mission-control style room improvised in the school's computer lab; five friends; screens with orbits]
Monday. The school computer lab, after dark. Kai has hacked nothing, but has borrowed every single screen.
On every screen, the same thing. The rock, and its path.
The rock is still coming. But now you have a word on a whiteboard. Nudge.
You can't catch it, Kai says. Bo looks offended.
So what if you had to stop an asteroid? Not in a movie. The real way.

## Why you can't catch it
[newspaper-style card; Bo with her arms out]
Think about it. The rock weighs about three and a half billion kilograms, and it moves twenty kilometres every second.
Catching it means stopping all that energy at once. About a hundred and seventy megatons.
Bo's arms are strong. But a hundred and seventy megatons would turn Bo, the rock, and the town into vapour.
And blowing it up is no better. You'd turn one big rock into lots of smaller rocks, all still coming.
So the trick isn't stopping it. It's changing where it goes. A tiny bit. Very early.

## The nudge
[an orbit drawing; a small arrow; the path slowly bending]
Here's the magic. Change a rock's speed by one centimetre per second. Much slower than walking.
Do it ten years early, and that tiny push adds up to thousands of kilometres by the time it arrives.
Enough to miss the whole planet.
It's like steering a huge ship. Turn the wheel early, and a tiny turn is enough. Turn it late, and nothing can save you.
Push it late, and you need a giant shove. Push it early, and a nudge is enough.

## DART
[deep space; a small spacecraft racing towards a pair of asteroids; a flash]
That's exactly what NASA tested. They picked an asteroid called Didymos, which has its own little moon called Dimorphos.
The moon is about a hundred and fifty metres wide. The same size as your rock.
In 2022, the DART spacecraft, about the size of a small car, flew straight into it, at twenty-two thousand kilometres an hour.
NASA hoped to change the moon's orbit by at least seventy-three seconds.
It changed it by thirty-two minutes.
And here's the surprise. The rubble that blasted off the surface pushed the rock even more, like a rocket engine made of gravel.
Now a European spacecraft called Hera is on its way to look at the crash site up close.

## The gentle way
[a spacecraft hovering next to a rock, a soft glow between them]
Scientists have a gentler idea too. A heavy spacecraft parks next to the asteroid.
It never touches it. Its own gravity slowly tugs the rock off course.
It's called a gravity tractor. One idea is that a twenty-tonne spacecraft could move a two-hundred-metre rock, if it had about twenty years.
It's slow. But it doesn't need to hit anything.
Ravi likes this one. It sounds like what he does, just slower.

## The practice
[the football pitch at dusk; a football rolling by itself]
So you practise. On the school football pitch, Ravi pushes a football without touching it.
Not hard. Just a tiny push, for a long time. The ball rolls the length of the pitch.
Then Ravi tries again with his eyes closed. Then with a bigger ball. Then with Bo's motorbike.
Bo gets bored and kicks the football into the next street. Kai makes her go and get it.
By the end of the night, Ravi can keep a gentle push going for as long as you like. The problem is, you don't have ten years.
You have three weeks. That's where you come in.

## The real thing
[night; the football pitch; the five friends lying on the grass; the rock bright in the sky]
That night, the five of you lie on the grass in the middle of the pitch.
Nia holds Ravi's hand, so he doesn't float away. Bo holds Nia's.
Kai counts down. Ravi raises one hand towards the sky. And you stop time.
The world freezes. A bird hangs in the air above the goal. Kai is stuck halfway through a blink.
But you reach out and touch Ravi's shoulder, and he can move too.
For everyone else, it's one second. For Ravi, it's a whole hour of gentle pushing. Like a gravity tractor with headphones.
An hour of pushing, three weeks early. You hope it's enough.
Time starts again. The bright dot in the sky doesn't look any different.
Nobody breathes. Kai is checking the numbers.
Then Kai smiles.
It's going to miss.

## After
[weeks later; a crowd on a hill watching a bright dot cross the sky]
A few weeks later, the rock flies past Earth. Millions of people go outside to watch it cross the sky.
Nia watches from above the crowd, where nobody looks. Bo carries the folding chairs.
The news calls it a lucky miss.
Nobody knows that five teenagers moved it. Kai finally opens the crisps.
