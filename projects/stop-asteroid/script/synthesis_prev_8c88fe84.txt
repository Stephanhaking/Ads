Catching it means stopping all that energy at once. About a hundred and seventy megatons. Bo's arms are strong. But a hundred and seventy megatons would turn Bo, the rock, and the town into vapour. So the trick isn't stopping it. It's changing where it goes, a tiny bit, very early.

Here's the magic. Change a rock's speed by one centimetre per second. Much slower than walking. Do it ten years early, and that tiny push adds up to thousands of kilometres by the time it arrives. Enough to miss the whole planet. Push it late, and you need a giant shove. Push it early, and a nudge is enough.

That's exactly what NASA tested. In 2022, the DART spacecraft flew into Dimorphos, a small moon of a bigger asteroid. It hit at twenty-two thousand kilometres an hour. The moon's orbit got thirty-two minutes shorter. NASA had hoped for seventy-three seconds. And the rubble it blasted off pushed the rock even more. Now a European spacecraft called Hera is on its way to see the crash site up close.

Scientists have a gentler idea too. A heavy spacecraft parks next to the asteroid. It doesn't touch it. Its own gravity slowly tugs the rock off course. It's called a gravity tractor. It's slow. But with enough years, it works.
