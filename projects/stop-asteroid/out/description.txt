What If You Tried to Stop an Asteroid?

The rock is coming, and the five friends have one word on a whiteboard: nudge. In this series finale they find out why you can't catch an asteroid or blow it up, why a push slower than walking is enough if it's early, how NASA's DART mission really moved an asteroid, and what a "gravity tractor" is. Then, on the school football pitch, they try it for real.

The story (and the powers) are fiction. The science and the missions are real (sources below).

THE SCIENCE AND WHERE IT COMES FROM
- A 150 m rock (about 3.5 billion kg) at 20 km/s carries roughly 170 megatons of TNT (our estimate); the biggest bomb ever tested was about 50 Mt
- Changing an asteroid's speed by about 1 cm/s ten years ahead moves its arrival point by roughly 3 x dv x t, about 9,000 km, more than Earth's radius (our rough estimate; real numbers depend on the orbit)
- 26 Sept 2022: NASA's DART spacecraft hit Dimorphos, the ~150 m moon of asteroid Didymos, at about 22,500 km/h; its orbit shortened by about 32 minutes (11 h 55 min to 11 h 23 min), against a minimum goal of 73 seconds; debris thrown off added to the push (Science News; Space.com; Scientific American)
- ESA's Hera spacecraft launched on 7 Oct 2024 to study Dimorphos after the impact (The Planetary Society; Space.com)
- Gravity tractor (Lu & Love, 2005): a spacecraft hovering near an asteroid tugs it with its own gravity; a 20-tonne tractor could deflect a typical 200 m asteroid given about 20 years (arXiv astro-ph/0509595; ESA)

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; not every original paper was read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
