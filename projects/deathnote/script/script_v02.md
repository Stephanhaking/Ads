## Cold open (0:00)
[a laundromat at 11 pm; a lost-and-found box on top of a dryer; a black notebook; rain on the window]
It's eleven at night, and you're the last person in the laundromat. On top of the lost-and-found box there's a black notebook with two words on the cover: Death Note.
Inside, the first page is a list of rules, written in careful English. The first one says: the human whose name is written in this note shall die.
You laugh. Then you read the rest. Then you stop laughing.
In the story, this notebook belongs to the shinigami, the gods of death, and it changes everything for a high-school student called Light. You're not Light. You've just got the notebook, a wet bag of laundry, and a choice you're going to have to make every single day.

## Day 1: the rules (0:50)
[your kitchen table at night; the notebook open under a lamp; you reading, not writing]
You read the rules at your kitchen table until two in the morning.
If you write a name and nothing else, the person dies of a heart attack forty seconds later. If you write a cause, you have six minutes and forty seconds to add the details.
And you have to know their face. A name alone doesn't work. You have to picture the person while you write.
You close the notebook. You don't write anything.
Rule one: a name is not enough. The notebook needs a face.

## Day 2: the test you don't do (1:40)
[a cafe; you with the notebook in your bag; a TV in the corner showing the news]
Everybody who hears this story asks the same question: how do you know it's real?
In the manga, Light tests it straight away. You don't. You sit in a cafe with the notebook in your bag, and every time someone is rude to the waiter, you feel it there, like a weight.
You realise something that isn't in any rule: the notebook doesn't need you to use it to change you. Just having it changes how you look at people.
Rule two: the most dangerous thing about the notebook is the idea of it.

## Day 5: the other notebook (2:30)
[a TV news report; a list of names on screen; city at night; your face lit by the TV]
On day five, the news has a strange story. Three men, all on trial, all died of heart attacks on the same afternoon. The reporter calls it a coincidence.
You look at your notebook. It's closed. You didn't write anything.
Which means there's another one.
Rule three: you are not the only one.

## Day 9: the pattern (3:15)
[a doctor's office; charts on a screen; a line going up; you watching from a hospital waiting room]
By day nine, there are twenty-one heart attacks, and every single one is someone the news has talked about. And this is where the real world comes in.
In Britain, a doctor called Harold Shipman killed his own patients for years. An official inquiry first concluded he had killed 215 people, and later estimated about 250. What raised the first alarm wasn't a confession. It was other doctors, who noticed that his patients were dying unusually often.
Afterwards, statisticians argued that simply watching the death rates would have caught him years earlier.
Someone, somewhere, is about to start watching the death rates.
Rule four: a single death is a mystery. A pattern is evidence.

## Day 14: the world reacts (4:10)
[phones; hashtags; a crowd outside a courthouse; police tape; a press conference]
On day fourteen the whole city is talking about it. Some people are scared. Some people are cheering, and that scares you more.
The police hold a press conference. A detective explains that they are treating it as a series. They're looking at who died, when, and who knew their faces.
In real life, how good police are at solving murders depends a lot on the country. One study in Japan put the solved rate at about 95 percent. In the United States in 2023, it was about 52 percent. The numbers aren't counted the same way, but either way, a city full of heart attacks is not the kind of thing investigators ignore.
Rule five: the more you use it, the louder it gets.

## Day 17: your friend finds it (4:55)
[your flat; your best friend on the sofa; the notebook on the coffee table; you walking in with two coffees]
On day seventeen, your best friend comes over, and when you walk back in with two coffees, they're holding the notebook.
They're laughing. "Death Note? Is this a joke?" They flick through the rules out loud, doing a spooky voice.
You take it back, a bit too fast. They stop laughing.
"Wait. You're not, like, taking this seriously?"
You say no. You say it's a prop from a party. And for the first time in your life, you lie to your best friend without even thinking about it.
That night you realise the notebook has already cost you something, and you haven't even opened it.

## Day 20: the offer (5:05)
[your bedroom at night; a tall, thin, shadowy figure in the corner; only its eyes glowing]
On day twenty, something is standing in the corner of your room. Tall. Thin. Its eyes glow red.
It's the shinigami who owns your notebook, and it has an offer. It can give you the shinigami eyes. With them, you'd see anyone's name just by looking at their face. You'd know who the other owner is in a second.
The price is half of the life you have left.
In the story, someone takes that deal, and then takes it again, and each time it halves what's left.
You think about it all night. You say no.
Rule six: every shortcut in this notebook costs more than it saves.

## Day 23: the lie in the rules (6:00)
[the notebook open; a page with a rule in a different handwriting; your finger on it]
On day twenty-three you find a page you hadn't noticed. A rule in a different handwriting: if the owner doesn't write a name every thirteen days, the owner dies.
For a second, your heart stops.
Then you remember. In the story, that rule is fake. Light wrote it himself, to fool the police. Someone has done the same thing here, to scare whoever finds the notebook into using it.
You almost fell for it. That's the thing about a notebook like this. It isn't only deadly. It's persuasive.

## Day 26: the interview (6:35)
[a small police interview room; a tired detective; a coffee cup; a folder of printouts]
On day twenty-six, the detective calls you back. They want to talk in person.
The interview room is small and too warm. The detective has a folder full of printouts: every death, every date, every face that was on the news.
"You were very helpful on the phone," they say. "How did you know so much?"
You tell them you read the news. You tell them you noticed the pattern, like those doctors who noticed the death rates.
They look at you for a long time. Then they smile, slide a card across the table, and say: "If you notice anything else, call me first."
You walk out into the cold air with your heart pounding. Because you realise the detective is doing exactly what you did. They're watching the pattern. And one day, the pattern might include you.

## Day 30: the choice (6:45)
[a rooftop at dusk; the city below; on your phone, a photo of the person you're sure is the other owner]
On day thirty, you work it out. The other owner is someone you know. You have their name. You have their face, in a photo on your phone.
You could end this in forty seconds.
You sit on the roof with the notebook open on your knees and a pen in your hand for a long time. The city lights come on one by one.
And you realise that the moment you write that name, you become exactly the thing you were trying to stop.
You close the notebook. You go downstairs, and you call the number from the press conference. You tell the detective everything you know, except one thing: that you have a notebook too.

## Day 31: the cost (7:40)
[the laundromat again, late at night; you put the notebook into the lost-and-found box... then take it back out]
The next night you go back to the laundromat to put the notebook back where you found it.
You hold it over the box for a minute. And you can't let go. Because whoever finds it next might not close it.
So you keep it. In a drawer. Unopened.
That's the real cost of the Death Note. Not half your life. Not a heart attack in forty seconds. It's that once you know it exists, you can never stop being the person who decides not to use it, every single day.

## Ending question (8:25)
[the notebook in a drawer; the drawer closes; the laundromat sign flickers off]
So, if you found this notebook tomorrow: would you write a name, or would you keep it closed forever?
And which rule scares you most? Tell us in the comments.
