## Cold open
[school gym, dodgeball; the hero (red T-shirt) on one side, the friend with the blue cap on the other]
Dodgeball. Last one standing. Your friend winds up and throws, hard, straight at your face.
You flinch. You squeeze your eyes shut and think one word. Stop.
And everything does.
The ball is hanging in the air, ten centimetres from your nose. Your friend is frozen mid-laugh. A kid in the stands is frozen mid-sneeze.
And it is completely, perfectly silent.
So what if you could stop time? Not slow it down. Stop it. For everyone except you.

## The silence
[the frozen gym; the hero walks around the ball]
The first thing you notice is the quiet. No squeaking shoes. No shouting. Not even a hum.
That's because sound is air moving. When someone shouts, they push air molecules, which push the next ones, all the way to your ear.
Freeze the air, and there's nothing left to carry a sound. You clap your hands. Nothing.

## The wall
[close-up of air as tiny dots; the hero pushing]
Then you try to walk, and it's like walking into a mattress.
Air looks like nothing, but it's packed with molecules, normally zooming around at about five hundred metres a second. That's faster than a jet airliner.
They usually bounce out of your way. Frozen, they don't move at all.
Physicists who've thought about this say pushing through frozen air would take enormous force. You wouldn't be walking through air. You'd be shoving through a wall of it.

## The dark
[everything goes black; only the hero's outline]
And it gets worse. To see anything, light has to travel from the world into your eyes. A few tiny packets of light, called photons, have to land on the back of your eye.
If time stops, light stops too. Nothing new reaches you.
One philosopher of physics put it simply: if you really stopped time, you probably couldn't see a thing.
Silent, solid, and pitch black. Not a superpower. A prison.

## The bubble
[the gym again; a glowing bubble around the hero]
So, like every story that does this, let's cheat. Time stops everywhere, except in a little bubble around you.
Inside your bubble, the air moves, so you can breathe, hear and walk. Light inside it still works, so you can see.
Physicists will tell you there's no real science that allows this. But it's your power, so you get a bubble.
You gently push the ball away from your face. You step out of the way. Then you let go.
The ball sails past. Your friend's mouth drops open. How did you dodge that?
Reflexes.

## The rules
[the hero's bedroom at night, a notebook with rules]
You start testing it. And the bubble has rules.
It only reaches about an arm's length around you. Outside it, everything is frozen, including the air.
So there's no fresh air coming in. Just whatever is in your bubble and in your lungs.
Most people can hold their breath for somewhere between thirty seconds and a minute and a half. Trained freedivers can go more than eleven minutes.
But your brain can start to be damaged after about four minutes without oxygen.
So your superpower comes with a timer. And you can't hear it ticking.

## Stopping light for real
[a dark optics lab; a green laser beam into a small crystal]
And here's the thing. Scientists have actually stopped something. Light.
In 2013, a team in Germany fired a beam of light into a special crystal, then switched off a second laser.
The light stopped inside the crystal. For about a minute. Then they switched the laser back on, and the light came out again, carrying a tiny picture of three stripes.
And in 2018, a camera at Caltech filmed at ten trillion frames a second. Fast enough to watch a pulse of light move.
Your own brain even does a small version. Look quickly at a clock with a second hand, and the first second can seem to last longer than the others. It's called the stopped-clock illusion.

## The crossing
[a busy city crosswalk; the friend's little brother runs after a ball into the road; a delivery van]
Then one Saturday, it matters.
Your friend's little brother chases a ball into the road. There's a van. It's too close.
You stop time.
Everything freezes. The van, the driver's face, the ball in mid-bounce.
You run. But your bubble only reaches arm's length, and every step you take, you're pushing your bubble through frozen air.
Ten seconds. Twenty. Your chest is burning. Forty.
You reach him. You grab his jacket. You pull him back to the pavement.
Fifty seconds. Your vision is going grey.
You let go.
The van roars past. The ball bounces off its bumper. And he's standing next to you, totally confused. Where did you come from?

## The other one
[the frozen street, a second time; a figure across the road]
That night, you try it one more time. Just a few seconds, to check you're okay.
The street freezes. Birds stuck in the sky. A cyclist stuck mid-pedal.
And across the road, someone is still moving.
They look straight at you. And they wave.
