What If You Could Turn Invisible?

You look in the mirror and you're gone. Then everything goes black. This story follows what invisibility would really be like: why a truly invisible person would be blind, why we aren't see-through already, the frog that hides its own blood, a food dye that made mouse skin transparent, the first real invisibility cloak, and why invisible is not the same as undetectable.

The story (and her visible eyes) is fiction. The science is real (sources below).

THE SCIENCE AND WHERE IT COMES FROM
- To see, the retina must absorb light; a perfectly invisible eye absorbs none, so a fully invisible person would be blind (standard optics; the argument goes back to H. G. Wells' The Invisible Man, 1897)
- We look solid because light scatters: water (refractive index ≈ 1.33), fats (≈ 1.46) and proteins (≈ 1.50) bend light by different amounts (Physics World; optics literature)
- Glass frogs: while resting they pack about 89 % of their red blood cells into the liver and become 2–3 times more transparent; how they avoid clotting is still unknown (Science, Dec 2022, Duke / AMNH; via Live Science, Discover)
- Tartrazine (FD&C Yellow 5) made the skin of living mice temporarily transparent; washing reversed it; not tested on humans, whose skin is much thicker (Science, Sept 2024, Stanford; via Stanford Engineering, Scientific American)
- First working invisibility cloak: Duke University with Imperial College London, Oct 2006 — a metamaterial cloak under 13 cm across that hid a copper cylinder from microwaves only (Science 314; Duke Today)
- A resting body gives off roughly 100 W of heat, and thermal cameras see people in total darkness
- Dogs have up to about 300 million smell receptors (breed-dependent) vs about 5–6 million in humans (Wikipedia, "Dog sense of smell"; receptor count isn't the same as "times better")

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; not every original paper was read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
