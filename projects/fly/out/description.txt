What If You Could Fly?

6 a.m. at the old observatory, and a girl drops out of the clouds hard enough to crack the paving stones. Nia can fly, and she's taking you up. This story looks at what flying would really mean for a human body: why we'd need enormous wings, how fast the air gets cold as you climb, and the real people who came closest: pedal-powered planes, wingsuits and a jet suit.

The story (and Nia's power) is fiction. The science and the records are real (sources below).

THE SCIENCE AND WHERE IT COMES FROM
- Flapping like a bird, a human would need wings about 6 m across (Michael Habib, via Live Science) and chest muscles about twice the size of a professional bodybuilder's, anchored to a keel bone (BBC Science Focus); estimates vary
- Birds devote a large share of their muscle mass to flight (BBC Science Focus)
- Air cools about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m on average (International Standard Atmosphere), so 3 km up is about 20 °C colder than the ground
- 12 June 1979: Bryan Allen pedalled the Gossamer Albatross across the English Channel, 35.8 km in 2 h 49 min; wingspan 28.6 m, empty weight about 32 kg (This Day in Aviation; FAI)
- 23 April 1988: Greek Olympic cyclist Kanellos Kanellopoulos pedalled MIT's Daedalus 88 115 km from Crete to Santorini in 3 h 54 min; a gust broke the tail and it fell into the sea about 7 m from the beach; still the record (MIT News; FAI)
- Wingsuits glide about 2.5 m forward for every 1 m down, at around 160 km/h or more (wingsuit training guides)
- 14 November 2019, Brighton: Richard Browning (Gravity Industries) set the jet-suit speed record at 136.9 km/h (85 mph) (Guinness World Records)

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; not every original paper was read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
