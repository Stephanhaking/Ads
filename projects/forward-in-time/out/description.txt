What If You Could Only Travel Forward in Time?

You blink in maths class and it's suddenly 3:15. You can skip ahead: a minute, a day, a year. But never back. This story follows what that power would really be like, and how real time travel into the future already works: speed, gravity, astronauts who landed a fraction of a second in the future, and what it would cost to skip years.

The story and characters are fiction. The physics is real (sources below).

THE PHYSICS AND WHERE IT COMES FROM
- Moving clocks run slow by the Lorentz factor; at 99 % of light speed, 1 year on board ≈ 7.1 years on Earth (our arithmetic from special relativity)
- 1971 Hafele–Keating experiment: four atomic clocks flown around the world on airliners lost 59 ns (eastward) and gained 273 ns (westward), matching predictions (Science, 1972; HyperPhysics)
- GPS satellite clocks: about −7 µs/day from speed, +45 µs/day from weaker gravity, net +38 µs/day; uncorrected, positions drift about 10 km a day (GPS World)
- Sergei Krikalev: 803 days in space in total, about 0.02 s "younger" (FAI; IOP Spark). In 1991–92 he went up for about five months, stayed about ten, and the Soviet Union dissolved while he was in orbit
- Fastest humans: Apollo 10, 39,897 km/h in 1969 ≈ 0.0037 % of light speed (Wikipedia; Guinness)
- Energy to bring 70 kg to 99 % of light speed: (γ − 1)mc² ≈ 3.8 × 10¹⁹ J ≈ 6 % of the world's yearly primary energy use, about 620 EJ in 2023 (our arithmetic; Energy Institute Statistical Review 2024)
- At a steady 1 g, reaching 99 % of light speed takes about 2.6 years of ship time (our arithmetic)
- Interstellar hydrogen (under 2 atoms per cm³) becomes deadly radiation near light speed; one estimate (W. Edelstein, 2010) put a lethal dose within about a second at 99.999998 % of c (press reports, Space.com / Popular Science)
- Gravitational time dilation: NIST (2010) measured a clock raised by 33 cm ticking faster; over a 79-year life that is about 90 billionths of a second (nist.gov)
- Backwards: general relativity allows closed timelike curves on paper; there is no known way to build one, and Hawking's "chronology protection conjecture" (1992) suggests physics may forbid them. Unproven.

These figures come from secondary sources and search summaries; not every original paper was read. If you spot an error, tell us in the comments.
