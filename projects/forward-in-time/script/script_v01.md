## Cold open
[classroom, afternoon; the hero (red T-shirt) at a desk, wall clock; friend with blue cap next to her]
It's two fifty-eight on a Friday. Maths. The clock on the wall is barely moving.
You stare at it and think, just be over already.
You blink.
And it's three fifteen. The classroom is empty. Your bag is packed. The bell rang, and you weren't there for it.
Your friend is in the doorway. Why are you just sitting there? You've been staring at the wall for twenty minutes!
You weren't staring. You were skipping.
Over the next few days you work it out. You can jump forward. A minute, an hour, a day. But never, ever back.
So what if you could only travel forward in time? Here's the twist. You already do.

## The rules
[home, kitchen; mum; the queue at the dentist; pizza]
At first, it's the best power in the world.
The dentist? Skip. The queue for the bus? Skip. Waiting for the pizza? Skip, and it's hot on the table.
Then your mum asks why your homework is never done.
And that's the catch. When you skip the boring part, you also skip the time you needed to do it.
Your friend tries to understand. Where do you go? Nowhere. I just get there later.
And that's exactly how real time travel works.

## The real way: speed
[runway and airliner; atomic clock; GPS satellite above Earth]
A hundred years ago, Einstein worked out something wild. The faster you move, the slower your clock ticks, compared with someone standing still.
It sounds like science fiction. So in 1971, two scientists put four atomic clocks on passenger planes and flew them around the world.
When they landed, the clocks no longer agreed with the ones that stayed behind. By billionths of a second. Exactly as Einstein predicted.
Your phone uses this every day. The clocks on GPS satellites drift by about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day.
Doesn't sound like much. But if engineers didn't correct for it, your map would be off by about ten kilometres by tomorrow.

## The time travellers
[space station; a cosmonaut; a map of a country changing]
Some people have actually travelled into the future.
Cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev spent eight hundred and three days in space.
Moving that fast for that long, he landed about two hundredths of a second in the future.
And on one trip, he went up in May 1991 for about five months. He stayed for ten.
While he was up there, the country that launched him, the Soviet Union, stopped existing.
He went up from one country, and came down to a different one.

## How fast is fast enough?
[the hero in a spaceship cockpit; Earth shrinking]
Two hundredths of a second isn't going to get you out of maths.
The fastest any human has ever travelled is Apollo 10, coming back from the Moon. Almost forty thousand kilometres an hour.
That's less than one two-hundredth of one percent of the speed of light.
To really skip ahead, you need to get close to light speed.
At ninety-nine percent of it, every year on your ship is about seven years back on Earth.
So you leave at thirteen. You come back a year older. Your friend is twenty.

## The cost
[cards; the spaceship; a glowing field of particles]
Here's the problem. Getting just your body to that speed takes about thirty-eight billion billion joules of energy.
That's around six percent of all the energy humanity uses in a whole year. Just for you. Not the ship.
Even pushing at a steady one g, it takes about two and a half years just to get up to speed.
And space isn't empty. There are one or two hydrogen atoms in every sugar-cube-sized bit of it.
Near light speed, those atoms hit you like a beam of radiation. One physicist calculated it could be deadly in about a second.
Honestly? Nobody has solved that yet.

## The other way: gravity
[two clocks on shelves; the hero standing next to a giant clock]
There's a second way to skip ahead. Gravity.
The closer you are to something heavy, the slower your time runs.
In 2010, scientists in the United States raised an atomic clock just thirty-three centimetres. And it ticked faster.
That means your head is ageing faster than your feet. By about ninety billionths of a second over a whole lifetime.
Don't worry. You won't notice.

## The big jump
[the lake at dusk; the pier; the hero alone, then the friend, older]
But you get greedy.
Exam week is coming. So you close your eyes, and you push. Harder than ever.
When you open them, the lake is the same. The pier is new.
Your phone has no signal. It won't even turn on.
Someone is standing at the end of the pier. Taller. A beard. A blue cap.
He stares at you like he's seeing a ghost.
You've been gone ten years.
Ten years of birthdays. Ten years of everybody looking for you.
And there's no button that takes you back.

## Can you ever go back?
[cards; a looping path of light]
So is backwards possible?
Einstein's equations allow some strange loops in time, on paper.
But nobody knows how to build one. Stephen Hawking even suggested that nature itself might forbid it.
It's an idea, not a proof. Right now, forward is the only direction we know works.
